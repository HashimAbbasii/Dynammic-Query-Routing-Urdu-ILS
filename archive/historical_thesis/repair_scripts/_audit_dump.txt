[68|Normal] Abstract
[69|Normal] Urdu information retrieval is difficult because users may type native Perso-Arabic script, informal Roman Urdu, or mixed forms, while many systems still apply one retrieval path to every query. This thesis proposes adaptive dynamic query routing for the ULTRA news-search setting. The official frozen system, M0, uses a Unicode script detector over a corpus of 111,860 cleaned Urdu news articles: URDU and MIXED queries search an Urdu BM25 index, and ROMAN queries search a Method D romanized-document BM25 index. Query-side variants M1–M4 did not improve the primary known-item score, so M0 was not replaced. On the Phase 2 development/validation known-item set, ExactSource Hit@5 is 68/78 = 87.18%. That figure is genuine for title-derived known-item search on that pool; it is not unseen usefulness and not overall system accuracy. Independent Phase 12 evaluation of the same freeze produced two further, separate results: ExactSource Hit@5 = 27/40 = 67.50% on new known-item queries (K001–K040), and human Success@5 = 23/40 = 57.50% on naturalistic queries (U001–U040; conservative P@5 = 0.2050, nDCG@5 = 0.6460, MRR = 0.4542). Success@5 is human relevance, not ExactSource Hit@5. These three percentages must not be averaged. In the U sample, Urdu-script queries succeeded in 17/18 cases versus 6/18 Roman and 0/4 mixed. The contribution is a frozen, reproducible script-aware retriever with strong controlled known-item performance and an explicitly measured Roman Urdu limitation.

[232|Heading 2] 1.1 Background
[233|Normal] Urdu is spoken by over 230 million people worldwide and serves as the national language of Pakistan and one of the officially recognized languages of India, yet it remains substantially underserved within contemporary information retrieval (IR) research (Daud et al., 2017). Unlike high-resource languages such as English, where dense retrieval, neural ranking, and large language model (LLM)-based query understanding have matured rapidly — driven by pretrained transformer encoders (Devlin et al., 2019) and dense dual-encoder retrievers (Karpukhin et al., 2020) — Urdu IR continues to face persistent challenges rooted in script complexity, morphological richness, and the informal, code-mixed nature of user-generated queries (Daud et al., 2017; Kazi & Khoja, 2025). A particularly acute challenge is the widespread use of Roman Urdu — Urdu written using the Latin script — in everyday digital communication and search queries, which introduces significant lexical and orthographic variation that conventional retrieval pipelines are not designed to handle (Hussain et al., 2025).
[234|Normal] The trajectory of information retrieval research over the past two decades helps situate why this gap has persisted for so long. Classical retrieval systems relied on lexical matching frameworks such as the probabilistic relevance model underlying BM25 (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009), which score documents based on term-frequency statistics rather than semantic meaning. These frameworks are language-agnostic in principle, but in practice they depend on well-tuned tokenization, stemming, and stop-word resources that were developed first — and most extensively — for English and a handful of other high-resource languages. The subsequent shift toward dense, embedding-based retrieval, exemplified by dual-encoder architectures such as DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020) and pretrained multilingual encoders (Conneau et al., 2020), promised to reduce this dependency by learning semantic representations directly from data. In practice, however, the quality of these representations is itself bounded by the volume and diversity of pretraining data available for a given language, and Urdu — despite its large speaker population — remains comparatively underrepresented in the web-scale corpora used to pretrain most multilingual models.
[235|Normal] This representational gap is compounded by a behavioral one. Empirical observation of Urdu-speaking users' search behavior suggests that a substantial share of real-world queries are not typed in the native Perso-Arabic script at all, but rather transliterated into Roman characters — a consequence of keyboard availability, typing familiarity, and long-standing digital communication habits that predate widespread Urdu-script keyboard support on mobile devices. Sitaram et al. (2019) frame this kind of script alternation as a broader instance of code-switching behavior, a phenomenon that conventional monolingual NLP pipelines, built around the assumption of a single consistent script, are structurally unequipped to handle. The consequence for Urdu information retrieval specifically is that any system evaluated only on native-script queries risks substantially overstating its real-world effectiveness, since it silently excludes a large and linguistically distinct subpopulation of actual user queries.
[236|Normal] The scarcity of standardized evaluation resources compounds this problem. Early efforts such as the Collection for Urdu Retrieval Evaluation (CURE) (Iqbal et al., 2021) and, more recently, the translated Urdu MS MARCO baseline (Butt et al., 2024), have begun to establish benchmarks for Urdu IR, but adaptive, query-aware retrieval strategies for Urdu remain largely unexplored. Where such strategies exist for other languages — for instance, classifier-driven routing between sparse and dense retrieval (Arabzadeh et al., 2021) or complexity-aware retrieval-augmented generation (Jeong et al., 2024) — they have been designed, trained, and validated exclusively on English data, leaving open the question of whether their underlying premise (that a lightweight, query-level classifier can reliably predict the best retrieval strategy) transfers to a morphologically richer, script-variable, lower-resource language such as Urdu.
[237|Normal] The ULTRA framework, proposed by Bashir, Qaiser, and Hussain (2026), represents a recent effort in this direction, introducing a dual-embedding, query-length-sensitive routing scheme that distinguishes between short, intent-oriented queries and longer, context-rich queries using a threshold-based decision process. However, ULTRA's routing mechanism relies on a static query-length threshold, which does not adapt to the full range of linguistic characteristics a real Urdu query may exhibit — including lexical diversity, Roman Urdu transliteration, and question structure. This mirrors a broader challenge identified in the general IR literature: Arabzadeh et al. (2021) showed that a learned classifier for routing queries between sparse and dense retrieval strategies can substantially outperform static rules, though their work was developed and evaluated only for English.
[238|Normal] This thesis extends ULTRA by treating script as the official routing decision for lexical news search. The frozen system M0 sends URDU and MIXED queries to Urdu BM25 and ROMAN queries to Method D. Earlier SVM SHORT/LONG work (two semantic rooms and confidence lights) is retained as historical Layer A. Official IR evaluation uses ExactSource Hit@5 and human Success@5, not development P@15 as the headline.
[239|Heading 2] 1.2 Problem Statement
[240|Normal] The practical problem is bilingual and script-mixed Urdu news search over 111,860 articles. Users issue native-script Urdu, Roman Urdu with non-standard spelling, and occasional mixed-script queries. A single Urdu-text BM25 index is a poor match for Roman queries (development Roman known-item Hit@5 was 0/23 without a Roman path). A static length threshold does not decide which script index to open. Classification accuracy on SHORT versus LONG is not the same as finding a useful news article.
[241|Normal] The scientific problem is therefore to freeze a retrieval system, report known-item recovery under a defined protocol, then report generalization on new known-item queries and naturalistic human usefulness without mixing those metrics. Chapter 4 specifies M0. Chapter 5 reports 68/78, 27/40, and 23/40 separately.
[242|Heading 2] 1.3 Motivation
[243|Normal] A search system that cannot decide which script index to open will miss Roman Urdu even when the article exists. The motivation is a frozen, inspectable BM25 pipeline with honest evaluation: development known-item, new known-item, and human usefulness kept apart. Overstating 87.18% as real-world accuracy would hide the Phase 12 gap.
[244|Normal] There is also a broader accessibility dimension to this motivation. Search and information-access tools are increasingly treated as basic digital infrastructure, and a system's usability for a given language community is only as strong as its weakest routing decision, since a single wrong routing choice can mean the difference between a user finding relevant results and abandoning the search entirely. For a language community as large as Urdu speakers, an IR system that only performs well on native-script input effectively excludes a substantial and demographically distinct segment of users — often younger, mobile-first users who default to Roman Urdu out of typing convenience rather than any deficiency in their command of the language. Improving routing reliability for Roman Urdu queries is therefore not merely a technical refinement but a step toward more equitable digital access for Urdu speakers as a whole.
[245|Normal] Finally, from a systems-engineering perspective, the choice between a lightweight, locally-executed classifier and an LLM-based router carries practical consequences that extend beyond raw accuracy. LLM-based routing, of the kind explored for retrieval-augmented generation by Jeong et al. (2024), introduces per-query inference latency and, in most deployment settings, a recurring monetary cost tied to API usage — both of which scale unfavorably with query volume. A classifier that matches LLM-level routing accuracy while running locally, at sub-millisecond latency and no marginal cost, is therefore not just an academic curiosity but a materially more deployable solution for real-world Urdu search infrastructure, particularly in resource-constrained settings where LLM API access may be unreliable, expensive, or unavailable altogether.

English query routing and Urdu collections exist; this freeze protocol did not.

[270|Heading 2] 1.9 Significance of the Study
[271|Normal] The significance of this study is best understood at three levels. At the linguistic and regional level, this work contributes one of the few dedicated treatments of Roman Urdu within a retrieval (rather than sentiment or offensive-language classification) context, extending the applicability of Urdu IR research to the code-mixed, informally written queries that plausibly constitute a large share of real-world search traffic from Urdu-speaking users. At the practical level, the efficiency profile of the proposed classifier — sub-millisecond inference at zero marginal cost — makes the findings directly actionable for practitioners building Urdu-language search or recommendation systems who must operate under real-world latency and cost constraints that LLM-based routing would violate.

[279|Heading 1] Chapter 2: Literature Survey
[280|Normal] Urdu information retrieval sits at the intersection of three research threads that are individually well studied for high-resource languages but remain fragmented for Urdu: general Urdu natural language processing, information retrieval benchmarking, and query-adaptive routing. This chapter reviews representative work in each thread, situates the proposed approach within the broader progression from lexical to neural to LLM-based retrieval methods, surveys the datasets and evaluation metrics relevant to this progression, and identifies the specific gap this thesis addresses.
[281|Heading 2] 2.1 Foundations
[282|Heading 3] 2.1.1 Urdu Natural Language Processing
[283|Normal] Daud et al. (2017) conducted a comprehensive survey of Urdu language processing, cataloguing the linguistic characteristics that complicate computational treatment of the language: a cursive, context-sensitive Perso-Arabic script written right-to-left, rich morphology, and the absence of large-scale standardized corpora compared to English or Arabic. Their survey remains a foundational reference for understanding why Urdu NLP tools lag behind those available for high-resource languages, though it predates the transformer era and therefore does not address embedding-based retrieval or query-adaptive systems.
[284|Normal] Kazi and Khoja (2025) more recently proposed a framework for Urdu language document retrieval, applying deep learning techniques and evaluating the effect of different word embeddings on retrieval-adjacent tasks such as named entity recognition. Their framework focuses on document-level representation quality rather than on how individual queries should be routed to different retrieval strategies based on their structural characteristics.
[285|Normal] A recurring theme across the Urdu NLP literature more broadly is that morphological and orthographic complexity does not merely add noise to a pipeline built for English — it changes which pipeline design choices are even viable. Techniques that work well for space-delimited, Latin-script languages, such as simple whitespace tokenization or suffix-stripping stemmers, transfer poorly to a cursive, context-sensitive script in which letter shape depends on position within a word. This has practical consequences for query routing specifically: a feature such as word_count, straightforward to compute for English, requires a tokenization decision for Urdu that is itself non-trivial, and the eight-feature representation adopted in this thesis (Chapter 3) was designed with this constraint in mind, favoring features that remain well-defined under simple whitespace tokenization rather than relying on a full morphological analyzer that does not yet exist at production quality for Urdu.
[286|Heading 3] 2.1.2 Roman Urdu Processing Challenges
[287|Normal] Roman Urdu — Urdu transliterated into the Latin script — lacks a standardized spelling convention, meaning the same word may be written in multiple ways depending on the writer's phonetic intuition. Hussain et al. (2025) addressed a related problem, fine-tuning large language models via QLoRA for offensive language detection in Roman Urdu-English code-mixed text, and explicitly identified inconsistent spelling, unstated grammar, and scarcity of labeled data as core obstacles for any Roman Urdu NLP task. Their work targets classification rather than retrieval, and does not address how Roman Urdu queries should be routed differently from native-script Urdu queries within an IR pipeline.
[288|Normal] More broadly, Sitaram et al. (2019) surveyed code-switched speech and language processing, characterizing the alternation between languages and scripts — of which Roman Urdu is a script-level instance — as a communicative phenomenon that conventional monolingual NLP pipelines are not designed to handle. Within Urdu specifically, Mehmood et al. (2020) tackled the closely related task of Roman Urdu sentiment analysis, proposing a hybrid multi-channel architecture over custom Word2Vec, FastText, and GloVe embeddings and reporting F1-score gains of up to 9% over adapted machine-learning baselines. Both works confirm that Roman Urdu requires script-aware, dedicated processing rather than being treated as a minor variant of native-script Urdu, but neither addresses query routing or retrieval.
[289|Normal] Taken together, this body of work suggests two broadly viable strategies for handling Roman Urdu within an NLP pipeline: (i) a dictionary- or rule-based transliteration approach that maps Roman Urdu tokens to their native-script equivalents before downstream processing, as adopted in this thesis, or (ii) an end-to-end learned approach in which a model is trained directly on Roman Urdu text without explicit transliteration, as in Hussain et al.'s (2025) QLoRA fine-tuning and Mehmood et al.'s (2020) custom embedding approach. The dictionary-based strategy was preferred here for two practical reasons: it requires no additional model training or labeled Roman Urdu corpus beyond the dictionary itself, and it produces a transliterated query that can be fed directly into the same native-Urdu embedding pipeline already used for the retrieval backend, avoiding the need to maintain two separate embedding spaces.
[290|Heading 3] 2.1.3 Low-Resource and Cross-Lingual Retrieval Limitations
[291|Normal] Urdu's challenges are not unique; a growing body of work shows that state-of-the-art dense retrieval degrades sharply once a language falls outside the small set of high-resource languages that dominate pretraining corpora. Wu et al. (2024) evaluated the multilingual Dense Passage Retriever (mDPR) — the retrieval component of the Cross-lingual Open-Retrieval Answer Generation (CORA) pipeline — on two extremely low-resource languages, Amharic and Khmer, and found that even after targeted extensions using translation language modelling and question-passage alignment, retrieval quality improvements were modest and absolute performance remained low. Their finding that language alignment techniques only partially close the gap for low-resource languages is directly relevant to Urdu: general-purpose multilingual dense retrievers cannot be assumed to transfer well without language-specific adaptation.
[292|Normal] Closer to the transliteration problem this thesis addresses, Chari et al. (2025) observed that current search systems are not usually trained on low-resource language varieties, forcing users to express queries in a high-resource language instead — a pattern directly analogous to Urdu speakers defaulting to Roman (Latin-script) transliteration when native-script input is inconvenient. They proposed fine-tuning neural rankers on pairs of related language varieties so that the ranker learns to exploit shared lexical and grammatical structure, improving retrieval effectiveness for the trained varieties.
[293|Normal] Conneau et al. (2020) showed that scaling multilingual pretraining (XLM-R) improves Urdu XNLI accuracy by 11.4 points over prior multilingual models, and that low-resource languages such as Urdu benefit disproportionately from scale — yet this gain is realized at the level of a single monolithic embedding model applied uniformly to every query, with no mechanism for detecting when a given query's script, length, or lexical structure would be better served by a different retrieval strategy altogether. Together, these studies confirm a consistent pattern across unrelated low-resource settings: general-purpose neural retrieval degrades for under-represented languages and scripts, and closing the gap requires language- or script-specific engineering rather than relying on multilingual pretraining alone. This motivates the explicit Roman Urdu detection and dictionary-transliteration module used in this thesis's methodology.
[294|Heading 3] 2.1.4 Information Retrieval Benchmarking for Urdu
[295|Normal] Iqbal et al. (2021) introduced CURE (Collection for Urdu Retrieval Evaluation), a standard test collection of Urdu documents built specifically to enable rigorous IR evaluation, addressing the absence of a shared benchmark comparable to TREC-style collections available for English. CURE provides an evaluation resource but does not itself propose an adaptive retrieval or routing mechanism.
[296|Normal] Butt et al. (2024) established the first large-scale Urdu IR dataset by machine-translating the MS MARCO passage ranking dataset into Urdu, and benchmarked both BM25 and fine-tuned multilingual transformer (mT5-based mMARCO) baselines, reporting a Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR@10) of 0.247 for their best fine-tuned configuration. Their evaluation applies a single retrieval strategy uniformly to all queries; no mechanism is proposed for detecting which queries would benefit from a different retrieval configuration.
[297|Heading 2] 2.2 Comparative Analysis of Retrieval and Routing Paradigms
[298|Normal] The methods surveyed in Section 2.1 and Section 2.6 span three broad technical paradigms — classical statistical/lexical methods, classical machine learning classifiers, and deep learning or LLM-based approaches — each carrying distinct trade-offs relevant to the query-routing problem addressed in this thesis. This section compares these paradigms explicitly, since the choice of an RBF-kernel SVM for the proposed routing classifier (rather than a lexical rule, a deep neural classifier, or an LLM prompt) was made deliberately in light of these trade-offs, rather than as a default choice.
[299|Heading 3] 2.2.1 Lexical and Statistical Methods
[300|Normal] The earliest and still most widely deployed retrieval paradigm relies on lexical term-matching, exemplified by the BM25 ranking function (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009), which scores document relevance from term-frequency and inverse-document-frequency statistics without requiring any learned representation. Applied to query routing rather than document ranking, an equivalent lexical strategy would route queries using simple surface statistics — precisely what the static, character-length threshold in the base ULTRA framework does. This paradigm's principal advantage is transparency and near-zero computational cost; its principal limitation, as demonstrated empirically in Chapter 5, is that surface statistics alone are insufficient to separate queries whose true routing requirement depends on script type or lexical structure rather than length alone.
[301|Heading 3] 2.2.2 Classical Machine Learning Approaches
[302|Normal] Between purely lexical rules and deep neural networks lies a family of classical machine learning classifiers — logistic regression, decision trees, and kernel methods such as the Support Vector Machine (Cortes & Vapnik, 1995) — that learn a decision boundary over a fixed, hand-engineered feature representation. This is the paradigm adopted in this thesis: an RBF-kernel SVM trained over the eight-dimensional feature vector described in Chapter 3. The appeal of this family, relative to both lexical rules and deep learning, is threefold: it requires substantially less training data than a deep neural classifier (the 409-query training set used here would likely be insufficient to train a competitive neural classifier from scratch), it produces calibratable confidence scores via Platt scaling without requiring a separate calibration architecture, and it executes at sub-millisecond inference latency on commodity hardware, without requiring a GPU.
[303|Heading 3] 2.2.3 Deep Learning and Neural Ranking Approaches
[304|Normal] Deep learning approaches to retrieval and classification, typically built on pretrained transformer encoders such as BERT (Devlin et al., 2019) and its multilingual variants (Conneau et al., 2020), learn representations directly from raw text rather than from hand-engineered features. Arabzadeh et al. (2021), for instance, trained a BERT-based classifier for sparse-versus-dense retrieval routing, and dense retrieval itself — as in DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020) and Sentence-BERT (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) — relies on transformer encoders to produce the embeddings compared during retrieval. The retrieval backend inherited from the base ULTRA framework in this thesis already uses this paradigm (via paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 embeddings, Section 4.2). For the routing decision specifically, however, a full transformer-based classifier was judged disproportionate to the task: routing is a comparatively low-dimensional decision (in this thesis, effectively binary, short-vs-long, augmented with confidence tiering) relative to the open-ended semantic matching that dense retrieval must perform, and the accuracy ceiling on this routing task (Chapter 5) is reached by the much lighter SVM classifier without requiring transformer-scale representations.
[305|Heading 3] 2.2.4 Large Language Model-Based Approaches
[306|Normal] The most recent paradigm applies large language models directly to routing and retrieval-adjacent decisions through prompting, rather than through a separately trained classifier. Jeong et al.'s (2024) Adaptive-RAG and Hsu and Tzeng's (2025) DAT both fall into this category, using an LLM to assess query complexity or to calibrate retrieval fusion weights at inference time. This paradigm's chief advantage is flexibility: it requires no dedicated training data or model training step, since the LLM's general-purpose reasoning is applied directly to the routing decision through a prompt. Its chief disadvantages, quantified directly in this thesis's evaluation (Chapter 5, Section 5.3), are per-query inference latency (hundreds of milliseconds, versus sub-millisecond for a local classifier) and, in most deployment settings, a recurring monetary cost tied to API usage that scales with query volume — both of which are avoided entirely by the SVM-based approach adopted here.
[307|Caption] Table 1. Comparative summary of retrieval/routing paradigms across cost and capability dimensions.
[308|Normal] This comparison motivates the specific design choice defended empirically in Chapter 5: the classical machine learning paradigm (Section 2.2.2) occupies a favorable position on the cost-capability trade-off for the query-routing task specifically — substantially more capable than lexical thresholding, while avoiding the training-data and inference-cost overhead of deep learning and LLM-based alternatives.

[313|Heading 2] 2.4 Evaluation Metrics in Information Retrieval
[314|Normal] Evaluating an IR system with a routing component requires metrics at two distinct levels: routing-decision quality and retrieval-result quality. This thesis reports both, and this section briefly surveys the metrics used and their provenance in the literature.
[315|Normal] At the routing-decision level, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score (Equations 3.8-3.11) are standard classification metrics applicable whenever routing is framed as a discrete decision, as it is in this thesis and in Arabzadeh et al.'s (2021) sparse-versus-dense classifier. Because the SVM classifier additionally produces calibrated confidence scores via Platt scaling, this thesis also reports the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and its Area Under the Curve (AUC), a threshold-independent measure of separability between classes; the importance of properly calibrated (rather than merely high) confidence scores for this kind of reporting is discussed at length by Guo et al. (2017), whose finding that modern classifiers tend toward systematic overconfidence directly motivated the use of Platt scaling (Section 3.4) rather than reporting raw decision-function margins as confidence.
[316|Normal] At the retrieval-result level this thesis uses two official IR metrics that must not be mixed: ExactSource Hit@5 (known-item recovery of a pre-assigned source document) and human Success@5 (at least one A or B in the Top-5). Development notebooks also reported P@15 for a small Layer A retrieval experiment; that P@15 is not official M0 performance. Mean Reciprocal Rank is reported for U as a secondary usefulness statistic.
[317|Normal] Finally, because a central claim of this thesis concerns deployment practicality relative to LLM-based routing (Section 2.2.4), two efficiency metrics not traditionally reported in retrieval-accuracy-focused IR literature are also measured directly in Chapter 5: per-query inference latency and per-query monetary cost. These are not standard IR metrics in the way P@k or MRR are, but they are essential to substantiating the practical, rather than purely academic, motivation for this thesis (Section 1.3).
[318|Heading 2] 2.5 Current Research Trends
[319|Normal] Three trends visible across the literature reviewed in this chapter are worth drawing out explicitly, since they collectively motivate the specific architecture proposed in this thesis. First, retrieval-augmented generation has emerged as the dominant paradigm for combining parametric language models with non-parametric retrieval (Lewis et al., 2020), and a growing sub-thread within this paradigm — adaptive or complexity-aware RAG (Jeong et al., 2024) and dynamic hybrid-fusion weighting (Hsu & Tzeng, 2025) — has moved away from applying a single fixed retrieval strategy to every query, toward query-dependent strategy selection. The three-tier confidence routing architecture proposed in this thesis is a direct instance of this broader trend, applied for the first time, to the authors' knowledge, to Urdu.
[320|Normal] Second, the multilingual NLP literature has increasingly recognized that scaling pretraining data and model size (Conneau et al., 2020) helps but does not fully resolve the performance gap for low-resource languages and scripts, motivating a parallel trend toward targeted, language-specific engineering — explicit transliteration modules, curated dictionaries, and script-detection components — rather than relying solely on ever-larger multilingual models (Chari et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024). The Roman Urdu transliteration module developed in this thesis (Section 4.3) is positioned within this second trend.
[321|Normal] Third, and more broadly across machine learning, there is growing scrutiny of the assumption that larger, more general models are unconditionally preferable to smaller, task-specific ones, particularly once deployment cost and latency are accounted for alongside raw accuracy. The direct efficiency comparison conducted in this thesis (Chapter 5, Section 5.3) between a lightweight SVM classifier and LLM-based routing baselines contributes an Urdu-specific, routing-specific data point to this broader ongoing discussion.
[322|Heading 2] 2.6 Prior Work
[323|Normal] Outside the Urdu-specific literature, query routing between retrieval strategies has been studied more extensively for English. The underlying premise — that queries differ systematically in how difficult they are to satisfy, and that this difficulty can be estimated before or during retrieval — was formalized early by Carmel and Yom-Tov (2010) in their synthesis of query performance prediction (QPP) methods, which surveyed pre- and post-retrieval signals for anticipating when a query is likely to fail. Classical QPP estimates how well a fixed retrieval strategy will perform on a given query, but does not itself select among alternative retrieval strategies or scripts, and was not developed with Urdu or code-mixed queries in mind.
[324|Normal] Arabzadeh et al. (2021) proposed a classifier that selects between sparse (BM25), dense, and hybrid retrieval strategies on a per-query basis, training a BERT-based model to predict which strategy would be most effective for a given query before retrieval is performed. Their approach reduced computational cost while maintaining retrieval effectiveness, by routing “easy” queries to the cheaper sparse retriever and reserving the dense retriever for queries it is more likely to help. This routing framework was designed and evaluated exclusively on English MS MARCO data; it does not address Urdu script variation, Roman Urdu transliteration, or confidence-tiered escalation.
[325|Normal] Building on the retrieval-augmented generation paradigm introduced by Lewis et al. (2020) — in which a parametric generator is conditioned on passages retrieved from a non-parametric index rather than relying on retrieval alone — Jeong et al. (2024) proposed Adaptive-RAG, a framework in which a lightweight classifier, trained on automatically collected complexity labels, predicts how complex an incoming question is and routes it to one of three retrieval-augmented generation strategies: no retrieval for the simplest queries, single-step retrieval for moderately complex queries, and iterative multi-step retrieval for the most complex queries. This three-way, classifier-driven escalation strategy is structurally analogous to the three-tier confidence routing mechanism proposed in this thesis, though Adaptive-RAG operationalizes complexity as a property of the question's reasoning depth rather than a calibrated classifier confidence score, and its classifier is a fine-tuned language model rather than a lightweight, low-latency SVM.
[326|Normal] Also relevant to the hybrid-search tier of this thesis's routing design, Hsu and Tzeng (2025) proposed DAT (Dynamic Alpha Tuning), a hybrid retrieval framework that abandons static dense/sparse fusion weights in favor of a per-query weighting factor, computed by having a large language model score the effectiveness of each retriever's top candidate before calibrating the fusion weight accordingly. Their central finding — that fixed hybrid-fusion weights are consistently suboptimal — provides independent support for the design choice, in this thesis's MEDIUM-confidence tier, of invoking hybrid search only adaptively rather than as a fixed default strategy. However, their LLM-based scoring adds non-trivial latency and cost per query, which is impractical at the throughput this thesis targets.
[327|Normal] Most directly relevant, Bashir, Qaiser, and Hussain (2026) proposed ULTRA: a dual-embedding Urdu architecture that already distinguished title/headline-level from full-content search using a length threshold, with precision gains above 90% on a large Urdu news corpus. ULTRA’s switch is still a static length tape. It does not learn headline-enough versus need-the-article, does not use script or why/how/fact cues, and does not turn confidence into mix-or-expand lights. This thesis keeps ULTRA’s two-level idea and replaces the tape with a learned dual-index router.

Collectively, the reviewed literature reveals three converging gaps that, individually, have each been addressed in isolation but never jointly, and never for Urdu. First, Urdu-specific NLP and IR resources have matured considerably, but remain either pre-neural in scope or focused on document-level representation and benchmarking rather than query-level adaptivity. Second, work on low-resource and cross-lingual retrieval confirms that general-purpose multilingual embeddings degrade for under-represented languages and scripts, motivating explicit, language-specific engineering. Third, query-adaptive routing has been repeatedly shown effective in high-resource-language IR — yet every one of these systems is evaluated exclusively on English and offers no mechanism for script-mixed or transliterated queries. [341|Heading 3] 3.1.1 Rationale for Feature Selection
[342|Normal] The eight V2 features in Table 3.1 were chosen to remain computable in a single pass over the query, without a tagger, parser, or extra model call. x₁-x₄ encode script composition (Urdu-character ratio, dictionary-based Roman ratio, and the corresponding binary flags), directly addressing the native-versus-Roman distinction in Section 1.2. x₅-x₆ encode length in words and characters, generalizing ULTRA's single scalar. x₇ flags mixed-script queries for future coverage; it is currently a dead feature. x₈ counts Urdu characters in absolute terms, complementing the ratio. An earlier development set used lexical diversity and question-word flags; those remain historically informative (Section 5.4) but are not inputs to the frozen V2 model.
[343|Heading 2] 3.2 Feature Standardization
[344|Normal] The eight-dimensional feature vector x(q) is standardized using a fitted feature scaler,
[345|Normal] z(q) = (x(q) − μ) / σ
[346|Normal] (3.2)
[347|Normal] Standardization is not a cosmetic step: the eight V2 raw features span markedly different numeric ranges (ratios, binaries, word count, character length, and an absolute Urdu-character count). An RBF kernel's distance computation (Section 3.3) is sensitive to relative scale. Without standardization, char_len and urdu_chars would dominate the kernel purely as an artifact of numeric range, drowning out ratio and binary features regardless of their true discriminative value. The fitted scaler is the deployed models/scaler.pkl object verified in Phase 3A.
[348|Heading 2] 3.3 SVM Decision Function
[349|Normal] The standardized vector z(q) is passed to a trained Support Vector Machine classifier (Cortes & Vapnik, 1995), whose decision function takes the general kernelized form
[350|Normal] f(z(q)) = Σᵢ αᵢ yᵢ K(z(q), zᵢ) + b
[351|Normal] (3.3)
[352|Normal] where zᵢ are the support vectors identified during training, αᵢ their learned dual coefficients, yᵢ their class labels, b the learned bias term, and K(·,·) the kernel function. The classifier uses a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel with probability estimates enabled via Platt scaling, and a fixed random seed (random_state = 42) for reproducibility. Regularization (C) and kernel coefficient (γ) are left at scikit-learn defaults (C = 1.0, γ = 'scale'). The predicted routing class is
[353|Normal] ŷ(q) = sign( f(z(q)) )
[354|Normal] (3.4)
[355|Normal] for the binary case, generalized to arg-max over class scores for the multi-class setting.
[356|Heading 3] 3.3.1 Why an RBF Kernel
[357|Normal] Three kernel choices were considered for the SVM: linear, polynomial, and RBF. A linear kernel restricts the decision boundary to a hyperplane in the original eight-dimensional feature space, appropriate only if SHORT/LONG are linearly separable there — a strong assumption given that V2 mixes ratios, binaries, and raw counts. A polynomial kernel adds a degree hyperparameter prone to overfitting on 409 training queries. The RBF kernel captures non-linear boundaries without that degree parameter. Gamma was left at scikit-learn's 'scale' default. Development-stage accuracy is robust across a wide C range (Section 5.7); generalization is assessed separately on frozen Phase 3B (86.00%).
[358|Heading 2] 3.4 Confidence Calibration
[359|Normal] Beyond the discrete class label, the classifier produces a calibrated confidence score by applying a sigmoid (Platt scaling) transformation to the decision function output:
[360|Normal] C(q) = 1 / (1 + e⁻⁽ᴬ·ᶠ⁽ᶻ⁽ᵢ⁾⁾⁺ᴮ⁾) × 100%
[361|Normal] (3.5)
[362|Normal] where A and B are calibration parameters fitted during training, and C(q) ∈ [0, 100]% is the confidence score used to drive the tiered routing decision. Platt scaling was chosen because it directly fits the confidence estimate to hold-out label agreement rather than deriving it heuristically from the decision margin alone, which mitigates the general tendency of modern classifiers toward overconfidence (Guo et al., 2017).
[363|Normal] An alternative to Platt scaling considered during design was isotonic regression, a non-parametric calibration method that can, in principle, fit a more flexible calibration curve than the two-parameter sigmoid Platt scaling assumes. Isotonic regression was not adopted here for a practical reason specific to this thesis's dataset scale: it is a non-parametric method with a larger effective number of degrees of freedom, and is known to be more prone to overfitting the calibration curve itself when the calibration set is small — a meaningful risk given that even the 409-query training set leaves a comparatively small portion leaves a comparatively small held-out portion for calibration. Platt scaling's two-parameter sigmoid form is more conservative under these data constraints, at the cost of assuming the calibration curve is well-approximated by a sigmoid, an assumption the near-perfect separability of the routing task (Chapter 5, ROC-AUC = 1.000) makes relatively benign in this specific application.
[364|Heading 2] 3.5 Confidence-Based Three-Tier Routing Function
[365|Normal] The routing confidence score C(q) determines one of three adaptive search behaviors through the piecewise routing function
[366|Normal] R(q) = one room (HEADLINE if SHORT, FULL_CONTENT if LONG),  if C(q) ≥ 85%  (HIGH)
[367|Normal] R(q) = hybrid mix of HEADLINE and FULL_CONTENT ranks,  if 60% ≤ C(q) < 85%  (MEDIUM)
[368|Normal] R(q) = expand query, then hybrid mix of both rooms,  if C(q) < 60%  (LOW)
[369|Normal] (3.6)
[370|Normal] The 85% and 60% cuts are policy constants, not fitted on the frozen 40. HIGH means the SVM is trusted to open one room. MEDIUM means mix both rooms because the label may be wrong. LOW means the query is too thin, so a short expansion is appended and then both rooms are mixed. Lights change search, not the frozen 86/84 scoring rule. The frozen 40-query P@5 table compares one-room systems so rooms stay comparable.
[371|Caption] Algorithm 3.1. Dynamic Query Routing (end-to-end).
[373|Heading 2] 3.6 Retrieval Scoring Function
[374|Normal] HIGH confidence searches one room. MEDIUM and LOW mix two rooms built with the same encoder (paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2): a headline embedding matrix and the full-article Chroma collection. Similarity in each room is cosine:
[375|Normal] sim(e_q, e_d) = (e_q · e_d) / (‖e_q‖ × ‖e_d‖)
[376|Normal] (3.7)
[377|Normal] Each room returns a ranked list. HIGH uses one list. MEDIUM/LOW fuse the two lists with equal weight after min-max normalisation (LOW first appends a short expansion string). Development notebooks also report unjudged P@15. Frozen dual-index comparisons report graded P@5 because human (or protocol-confirmed) judgments stop at rank 5.
[378|Heading 2] 3.7 Evaluation Metrics
[379|Normal] The system is evaluated along two complementary dimensions: routing accuracy and retrieval relevance. For the routing task, standard binary-classification outcomes are defined relative to the routing decision, from which routing accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score are computed:
[380|Normal] Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + FP + FN + TN)
[381|Normal] (3.8)
[382|Normal] Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
[383|Normal] (3.9)
[384|Normal] Recall = TP / (TP + FN)
[385|Normal] (3.10)
[386|Normal] F1 = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall)
[387|Normal] (3.11)
[388|Normal] Official M0 retrieval quality is ExactSource Hit@5 (n=78 and K) and human Success@5 (U). Historical Layer A also reported development P@15 and frozen dual-index graded P@5 (Relevant = 1, Partially relevant = 0.5, Not relevant = 0):
[389|Normal] P@5 = (sum of graded relevance in the top-5 results) / 5
[390|Normal] (3.12)
[391|Normal] The routing classifier's average confidence across the evaluation set is additionally reported as
[392|Normal] C̄ = (1/N) Σᵢ C(qᵢ)
[393|Normal] (3.13)
[394|Normal] where N is the number of evaluation queries and C(qᵢ) is the per-query confidence score from Equation 3.5.
[395|Heading 2] 3.8 Computational Complexity Analysis
[396|Normal] Because deployment efficiency relative to LLM-based routing is a central claim of this thesis, it is useful to state the computational complexity of each pipeline stage explicitly. Feature extraction (Section 3.1) is O(|q|) in the length of the query string, since every one of the eight features can be computed with a single pass over the query's characters or whitespace-delimited tokens. Feature standardization (Eq. 3.2) is O(1) per feature, hence O(8) = O(1) overall, since it is a fixed elementwise affine transform. SVM inference (Eq. 3.3) is O(n_sv × d) where n_sv is the number of support vectors retained after training and d = 8 is the feature dimensionality; because n_sv is fixed at training time and does not grow with corpus size, this step's cost is constant with respect to the size of the underlying document corpus. The retrieval step itself (Eq. 3.7) is the only stage whose cost depends on corpus size, and even there the HNSW index reduces the expected query complexity from the O(|Corpus|) of an exhaustive nearest-neighbor search to approximately O(log|Corpus|) under typical HNSW graph-construction parameters. Table 3.2 summarizes these complexity bounds alongside the measured wall-clock latency reported in Chapter 5.

[405|Heading 2] 4.1 System Architecture
[406|Normal] The system architecture (Figure 4.1) illustrates the end-to-end pipeline, organized into four sequential stages: (i) language-variant normalization, (ii) feature-based query representation, (iii) confidence-calibrated routing, and (iv) tiered retrieval execution. Each stage is designed to be independently replaceable, so that the routing signal (Stages ii-iii) can evolve without requiring changes to the underlying retrieval backend (Stage iv).
[407|Normal] Stage 1 — Language-variant normalization. Roman Urdu is detected and transliterated with the expanded dictionary (198 on-disk pairs) before Stage 2; native-script Urdu bypasses transliteration.
[408|Normal] Stage 2 — Feature vector. V2 uses the eight Phase 3A features. The trap-augmented model appends four binary intent flags (causal, manner, synthesis, fact cue). Neither vector uses θ = 150 as an input feature.
[409|Normal] Stage 3 — Confidence-calibrated routing. x(q) is standardized using a fitted feature scaler and passed to the trained SVM classifier, which outputs both a routing decision ŷ and a calibrated confidence score C(q) ∈ [0, 100]%.
[410|Normal] Stage 4 — Two rooms and lights. HIGH: search only HEADLINE or only FULL_CONTENT. MEDIUM: hybrid both rooms. LOW: expand, then hybrid. This is the operational replacement of ULTRA's single combined-text search.

[420|Heading 3] 4.2.1 Corpus Preprocessing
[421|Normal] Prior to embedding, the raw corpus is passed through a preprocessing stage inherited from the base ULTRA framework, consisting of four sequential steps: (i) removal of HTML artifacts and non-textual boilerplate (bylines, publication timestamps, and navigation text) that would otherwise pollute the embedding signal; (ii) de-duplication of near-identical articles, which are common in news corpora due to wire-service syndication of the same story across multiple outlets; (iii) category normalization, mapping inconsistent or overlapping category labels in the raw dataset onto the four consolidated categories reported in Table 4.2; and (iv) minimum-length filtering, discarding articles too short to carry meaningful topical content (e.g., single-sentence stubs). This preprocessing pipeline is not itself a contribution of this thesis — it is inherited unchanged from the ULTRA framework, consistent with the scope defined in Section 1.8 — but is documented here for completeness, since the routing classifier's feature distributions (Chapter 5) are indirectly shaped by the characteristics of the corpus its retrieval component operates over.

[423|Normal] The resulting category distribution is visibly imbalanced, with Sports and Entertainment together accounting for over 71% of the corpus. [482|Normal] It is worth pausing on why the static baseline lands so close to 50.00% — essentially chance-level performance for a balanced binary decision — rather than at some intermediate accuracy. Because the query dataset was deliberately curated to contain queries whose true short/long label does not correlate with the θ = 150 cut-point (Figure 4.2), the static rule's predictions are effectively uncorrelated with the ground truth on this evaluation set; 50.00% is exactly what a coin-flip decision rule would be expected to score on a class-balanced dataset. This is a stronger and more informative failure mode than a baseline that merely underperforms — it demonstrates that the static rule's decision boundary is not just miscalibrated but structurally unrelated to the actual routing requirement for this query population, which is precisely the argument made analytically in Section 4.2 with respect to Figure 4.2.
[483|Normal] The fact that both the logistic regression and SVM classifiers independently reach exactly 100.00% test accuracy is also worth interpreting statistically rather than accepting at face value. Logistic regression assumes a linear decision boundary in the (standardized) feature space, while the SVM's RBF kernel permits an arbitrarily non-linear boundary; the two models converging on identical, perfect test accuracy suggests that the eight-dimensional feature space renders the short/long distinction linearly separable — or very close to it — for this dataset, since a linear model would not match a more flexible non-linear model's accuracy otherwise. This observation is consistent with, and helps explain, the ablation results of Section 5.5, where accuracy remains unchanged after removing any single feature: a linearly (or near-linearly) separable problem in an eight-dimensional space is unlikely to depend critically on any one dimension, since redundant near-collinear features (e.g., char_length and word_count) can each individually substitute for information the others also carry.

[508|Heading 2] 5.5 Ablation Study
[509|Normal] Table 5.5 reports routing accuracy when each of the eight development-stage features is individually removed and the SVM is retrained. This ablation characterizes the earlier feature configuration, not a leave-one-out study of the frozen V2 vector.
[510|Caption] Table 19. Ablation study: accuracy with each feature individually removed.
[512|Caption] Figure 10. Ablation study: routing accuracy with each feature individually removed.
[513|Normal] Every single-feature ablation on the development configuration retains 100.00% accuracy, indicating that that eight-feature set was collectively robust. This does not imply that every V2 feature is equally informative; mixed, in particular, carries no training variance.
[514|Normal] This apparent tension — a feature (has_question_words) being highly important by permutation analysis (Section 5.4) yet fully removable without any accuracy loss by ablation (this section) — is not a contradiction but a direct consequence of the two methods measuring different things. Permutation importance measures how much the model relies on a feature's specific values when all eight features are present together; ablation measures whether the model can be retrained to reach the same accuracy without that feature at all. A feature can be heavily relied upon within the full model while still being individually dispensable if other features carry enough of the same signal to substitute for it once the model is retrained. Practically, this means the eight-feature design is robust to the accidental loss or corruption of any single feature at inference time — a desirable property for a production system — without implying that all eight features are equally informative when interpreting the model's behavior.

[524|Normal] Effect size (Cohen's d). Figure 5.9 reports the standardized effect size of each feature in separating short from long queries. char_length (d=14.249) and query_length (d=11.832) show extremely large effects, alongside urdu_chars (d=1.634) and has_roman (d=0.524); the mean effect size across all measured features (d=3.581) is more than four times the conventional “large effect” threshold (d ≥ 0.8). Statistically, Cohen's d measures the standardized distance between the short-query and long-query group means for a given feature, in units of pooled standard deviation; a d of 14.249 for char_length is an extraordinarily large effect by the standards of most applied machine learning or social-science research, where d ≥ 0.8 is already considered a strong result, and indicates that the two query classes are separated by more than fourteen pooled standard deviations along this single dimension — a degree of separation that helps explain why both the linear (logistic regression) and non-linear (SVM) classifiers achieve identical, perfect accuracy (Section 5.1): the underlying classes are simply far apart in feature space, leaving little room for either model to disagree with the other or with the ground truth.

[542|Heading 2] 5.8 Error Analysis
[543|Normal] Although the development-stage routing classifier reached zero misclassifications on the primary test split (Section 5.1), the deployed V2 model is not error-free on later held-out data (98% on the 50-query diagnostic set; 86% on frozen Phase 3B). The retrieval-precision results (Section 5.2) also contain non-trivial errors, examined here rather than folded into the aggregate P@15 figures.
[544|Normal] The two lower-scoring Roman Urdu queries — “mobile phone naya model” (86.67% P@15) and “punjab police corruption” (53.33% P@15) — share a common structural pattern: each combines a term with high cross-lingual ambiguity (“mobile phone”, an English loanword commonly used as-is within Roman Urdu text; “punjab”, a proper noun that is orthographically identical in Roman transliteration and in English) with otherwise ordinary Roman Urdu vocabulary. In both cases, the transliteration dictionary (Section 4.3) is not incorrect so much as under-specified: “mobile phone” and “punjab” are valid tokens whether interpreted as English or as Roman Urdu, and the dictionary lookup step (Algorithm 4.1) has no mechanism for resolving this ambiguity contextually, since it operates at the level of individual whitespace-delimited tokens rather than the query as a whole. This is best characterized as a lexical-ambiguity error rather than a routing error: the routing classifier correctly identified both queries as Roman Urdu and routed them appropriately (Section 5.6); the precision loss originates downstream, in the transliteration step's inability to disambiguate loanwords and proper nouns from genuine Roman Urdu vocabulary.
[545|Normal] This distinction matters for interpreting the overall system's error profile: further gains in Roman Urdu P@15 are more likely to come from improving the transliteration module (e.g., a context-aware or learned transliteration model, Section 6.3) than from treating routing accuracy as a solved problem. Routing is at ceiling on some development splits, but Phase 3B shows residual SHORT/LONG errors on unseen queries (SVM: 12/16 SHORT correct, 31/34 LONG correct). Native-script Urdu queries bypass transliteration (Algorithm 4.1, Line 11) and are therefore unaffected by the loanword/proper-noun dictionary failure mode.
[546|Heading 2] 5.9 Comparison with Prior Studies
[547|Normal] Situating these results against the prior work reviewed in Chapter 2 is useful both to calibrate how large the reported improvements are in absolute terms and to identify where direct numerical comparison is, and is not, methodologically appropriate.
[548|Normal] Relative to the base ULTRA framework (Bashir et al., 2026), the development P@15 comparison (43.75% to 90.00% overall in a small experiment) is Layer A only. Official M0 comparison on n=78 is ExactSource Hit@5 = 68/78 versus Urdu-only BM25 0.5897, not that P@15 table.
[549|Normal] Relative to Arabzadeh et al.'s (2021) English sparse-versus-dense routing classifier, direct numerical comparison is not meaningful, since their evaluation uses English MS MARCO queries and a different retrieval configuration entirely; however, the qualitative finding — that a learned classifier can route queries more effectively than a fixed rule, at lower computational cost than exhaustively applying every retrieval strategy — is corroborated independently by this thesis for an unrelated language, script, and routing decision space, lending some cross-linguistic generality to the underlying premise that motivated both works.
[550|Normal] Relative to Butt et al.'s (2024) Urdu MS MARCO baseline (MRR@10 of 0.247 for their best fine-tuned configuration), no direct numerical comparison is offered in this thesis, since P@15 and MRR@10 measure different aspects of retrieval quality (precision across a result set versus rank position of the first relevant result, Section 2.4) and the two studies evaluate against different corpora and query sets. The comparison is nonetheless useful contextually: Butt et al.'s comparatively modest MRR@10, achieved with a fine-tuned multilingual transformer applying a single retrieval strategy uniformly, is consistent with this thesis's broader argument (Section 1.2) that a substantial share of Urdu IR's remaining performance gap lies not in retrieval-model quality alone but in how queries are matched to the retrieval strategy best suited to them — the specific problem this thesis's routing layer addresses.
[551|Heading 2] 5.10 Strengths and Weaknesses of the Proposed System
[552|Normal] Consolidating the results above, three strengths and three weaknesses of the proposed system are worth stating explicitly, ahead of the limitations discussion in Chapter 6.
[553|Normal] Strengths. First, the routing task is learnable, and frozen V2 still generalizes to 86% rather than collapsing to chance. Second, sub-millisecond local inference is a real deployment fact against LLM-style routers. Third, Roman Urdu is no longer a zero-P@15 dead end in the development retrieval experiment. Fourth, trap labels show that a cue-aware SVM can beat a six-word tape on need (60% vs 20%), which θ = 150 cannot.
[554|Normal] Weaknesses. First, dictionary transliteration still fails on loanwords and proper nouns. Second, frozen V2 only just matches word count (86/84; p = 1.0). Third, trap gains are cue-word gains, not a general “need” detector (27.27% without cues). Fourth, dual-index P@5 on those traps does not beat word count (33.00% vs 36.50%). Fifth, development 100% and AUC = 1.000 must not be cited as the paper result.
[555|Heading 2] 5.11 Practical Implications
[556|Normal] Beyond the academic contribution of demonstrating that dynamic routing outperforms static thresholding for Urdu IR, the results in this chapter carry several implications for practitioners considering deployment of a similar system. The latency and cost comparison (Section 5.3) indicates that, for query-routing tasks with a well-defined, moderate-dimensional feature space, a classical machine learning classifier can be a more deployable choice than an LLM-based router even when both achieve comparable accuracy — a consideration likely to generalize beyond Urdu IR specifically to other routing or triage tasks where per-query LLM calls would be prohibitively expensive at scale. The error analysis (Section 5.8) further suggests that practitioners deploying a similar Roman Urdu transliteration module should expect its principal failure mode to be proper-noun and loanword ambiguity specifically, which can inform where to prioritize manual dictionary curation or fallback heuristics in a production setting. Finally, the confidence-tiered architecture (Section 5.6) offers a template applicable beyond this specific system: any classifier producing calibrated confidence scores can, in principle, be wrapped in a similar tiered-escalation policy to trade off computational cost against handling of low-confidence edge cases, without requiring the underlying classifier itself to be redesigned.
[557|Heading 2] 5.12 Discussion
[558|Normal] Taken together, Layer A shows that θ = 150 is the wrong length switch and that an SVM can learn SHORT versus LONG on development data, with only a small frozen classification edge versus word count (86% vs 84%) and no dual-index P@5 gain on H001–H040. That story is not the official retrieval evaluation. Official M0 results are in Sections 5.18–5.24: 87.18% ExactSource Hit@5 on the development/validation known-item pool, 67.50% ExactSource Hit@5 on new known-item queries, and 57.50% human Success@5 on naturalistic queries. Roman Urdu is the main usefulness gap (U: 6/18 versus Urdu 17/18). Development 92.50% P@15 and 90.00% overall P@15 remain supporting Layer A figures, not the M0 headline.
[559|Normal] Second, the proposed classifier matches the routing accuracy of three of four LLM-based routing baselines while operating at approximately three orders of magnitude lower latency (0.4555 ms vs. 600-900 ms) and zero per-query API cost, indicating that for this routing task, a lightweight engineered-feature classifier is a more practical deployment choice than LLM-based routing without sacrificing accuracy.
[560|Normal] Third, development P@15 of 92.50% on eight Roman queries (overall 90.00% in that experiment) is not official M0 performance. Official Roman evidence is Method D 22/23 on development title_roman known-items, K Roman ExactSource 1/12, and U Roman Success@5 6/18.
[561|Normal] Fourth, development permutation/ablation numbers describe an earlier feature set, not frozen V2, and must not be used to claim that question-words drive the deployed model. V2 is script and length; V3 adds cue flags. Collectively these findings answer RQ1–RQ3 with the limits stated in Chapter 6.

[583|Heading 2] 6.1 Conclusions
[584|Normal] This thesis evaluated Urdu news retrieval under a frozen lexical system (M0) after earlier work on learned SHORT/LONG routing. Official M0 uses Unicode script detection, Urdu BM25 for URDU/MIXED queries, and Method D for ROMAN queries over 111,860 articles. M0 was not changed after Phase 12. M1–M4 did not improve n=78 Hit@5.

ExactSource Hit@5 and Success@5 measure different properties and must not be averaged with each other or with 87.18%.
[586|Normal] ULTRA therefore demonstrates effective script-aware retrieval on the development/validation known-item benchmark, while new unseen evaluations reveal a substantial generalization gap and particularly weak Roman-script performance (U Success@5: Urdu 17/18, Roman 6/18, Mixed 0/4). The contribution is not 87% real-world accuracy. H001–H040 Success@5 = 62.5% is diagnostic only.

[597|Heading 2] 6.3 Limitations
[598|Normal] Limitations of the official M0 evaluation are stated first. Historical SVM-layer limits (Phase 3B power, HIGH-tier demonstration) remain valid for Layer A but do not replace the IR limits below.
[599|Normal] Unseen evaluation size. New known-item (K) and naturalistic (U) sets each have n = 40. Point estimates have wide uncertainty. Numerators and denominators must be reported. This evaluation should not be interpreted as universal real-world accuracy.
[600|Normal] Roman Urdu performance is substantially weaker than Urdu-script performance on the sealed U sample (6/18 versus 17/18). Ordinary and chat spelling diverge from Method D’s title_roman construction. The mixed-script subset was very small (n = 4, all unsuccessful) and cannot support a population rate. Known-item versus naturalistic evaluation. ExactSource Hit@5 requires a pre-assigned source document. Human Success@5 asks whether any Top-5 document is relevant or partially relevant. The drop from 87.18% to 57.50% is not one metric getting worse. Development/validation and unseen results differ for both scientific and sampling reasons. Naturalistic queries require human relevance judgments. U labels are from one annotator; there is no inter-annotator agreement. nDCG@5 with topical C-gain = 1 can look high when Success@5 fails and is not the usefulness headline.
[601|Normal] Single retrieval corpus. All experiments were conducted against one news-domain corpus (111,860 Urdu news articles). The classifier's features and decision boundary were learned in this domain, and its behavior on other domains (e.g., legal, medical, or social media text) has not been evaluated.
[602|Normal] Static LLM baselines. The comparison against GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Claude, and Gemini-style routing used fixed prompting strategies rather than fine-tuned or extensively prompt-engineered variants of these models, so the reported LLM baseline numbers should be read as indicative rather than an upper bound on what LLM-based routing could achieve.
[603|Normal] Binary routing granularity. The current classifier makes a routing decision from a fixed feature set computed once per query; it does not yet incorporate user session context, query reformulation history, or multi-turn conversational signals that could further refine routing in interactive search settings.
[604|Normal] Lights versus P@5 protocol. MEDIUM and LOW are implemented in code and shown on live examples. The frozen 40-query P@5 table compares one-room systems so that headline versus full lists remain comparable. Hybrid P@5 is therefore not claimed as a frozen number.
[605|Heading 2] 6.4 Recommendations for Practitioners
[606|Normal] Distinct from the research-oriented future work outlined in Section 6.5, the following recommendations are addressed specifically to practitioners considering adoption or adaptation of the methods developed in this thesis.
[607|Normal] Do not treat 87.18% ExactSource Hit@5 as a user-facing SLA. For native-script Urdu news on this collection, frozen M0 is a reasonable lexical baseline. For Roman Urdu traffic, expect a large drop (U Success@5 = 6/18 in the sealed sample). If the system is changed, freeze it first and seal a new test; K, U, and H001–H040 are burned for tuning.
[608|Normal] Audit transliteration dictionaries for proper-noun and loanword ambiguity. Section 5.8's error analysis identified proper nouns and English loanwords as the dominant Roman Urdu error source; practitioners building similar dictionary-based transliteration modules should specifically audit and, where possible, disambiguate these categories rather than treating dictionary coverage as a uniform quantity to simply expand.
[609|Normal] Keep ExactSource Hit@5 and human Success@5 separate. Do not average 87.18%, 67.50%, and 57.50%. Layer A SVM classification (86/84) is not official M0 retrieval performance.
[610|Heading 2] 6.5 Recommendations for Further Research
[611|Normal] Building on the contributions and limitations above, the following directions for future research are identified.
[612|Normal] Do not tune BM25, the dictionary, routing, or Method D on U001–U040, K001–K040, or H001–H040. Future Roman work needs a new sealed test. A larger independently annotated Urdu and Roman Urdu query benchmark would also enable more robust comparison than n=40.
[613|Normal] Learned or generative Roman Urdu transliteration. Rather than extending the transliteration dictionary manually, future work could train a sequence-to-sequence or transformer-based transliteration model on a larger parallel Roman Urdu-Urdu corpus, potentially closing the residual gap on proper-noun-heavy and out-of-vocabulary queries identified in Chapter 5.
[614|Normal] Cross-domain and multilingual generalization. Evaluating — and, where necessary, retraining — the dynamic routing classifier on corpora outside the news domain (e.g., government, legal, or educational text), and extending the approach to other low-resource South Asian languages that share Urdu's script and code-mixing challenges (e.g., Punjabi, Sindhi, Hindi-Urdu code-switched text), would test the generality of the proposed feature set beyond a single domain and language.
[615|Normal] Fine-tuned and prompt-optimized LLM routing. Since the current LLM comparison relies on fixed prompting strategies, a more rigorous comparison would fine-tune or systematically prompt-optimize LLM-based routers on the same training data used for the SVM classifier, providing a fairer accuracy ceiling against which the efficiency advantages of the lightweight classifier can be judged.
[616|Normal] Richer, context-aware feature sets. Incorporating session-level signals — such as prior queries in a search session, click-through feedback, or user-specific query history — into the feature set could allow the router to adapt its decisions dynamically within a single user interaction, moving from single-query classification toward session-aware retrieval routing.
[617|Normal] Priority directions that require a new sealed evaluation: better Roman query–document matching than Method D; mixed-script evaluation with more than four queries; a second annotator on a new naturalistic set.
[618|Normal] Deployment and real-world evaluation. Finally, integrating the proposed routing pipeline into a live Urdu search system and evaluating it against real user queries and engagement metrics (rather than offline precision measures alone) would validate whether the efficiency and accuracy gains demonstrated here translate into measurable improvements in real-world retrieval quality.
[619|Heading 2] 6.6 Real-World Applications
[620|Normal] Beyond the immediate Urdu news-retrieval setting evaluated in this thesis, the dynamic routing architecture developed here has plausible application in several adjacent, practically important settings. In consumer-facing Urdu and Roman Urdu search products — including general web search, e-commerce product search, and in-app content search for Urdu-language platforms — the routing layer could be deployed largely as-is, given its independence from the specific news-domain retrieval backend used in this thesis's evaluation (Section 1.8). In customer-support and helpdesk systems serving Urdu-speaking users, where queries are frequently typed in Roman Urdu under time pressure, the same routing-plus-transliteration architecture could reduce the proportion of queries that return no relevant results, directly improving user-facing service quality. In digital-library and government-portal search over Urdu-language documents, where query volume may be lower but computational budgets are often more constrained than in commercial search, the proposed system's zero-marginal-cost, sub-millisecond routing profile (Section 5.3) is particularly well suited relative to LLM-based alternatives that would incur recurring API costs disproportionate to typical public-sector search traffic. Realizing any of these applications would require the domain-adaptation and larger-scale validation work identified in Section 6.5, but the underlying architecture — feature-based routing plus confidence-tiered escalation — does not depend on assumptions specific to the news domain and is presented here as a general pattern applicable across these settings.
[621|Heading 2] 6.7 Closing Remarks
[622|Normal] This thesis measured script-aware Urdu news retrieval under a freeze. M0 achieves 87.18% ExactSource Hit@5 on the development/validation known-item protocol, 67.50% on new known-item queries, and 57.50% human Success@5 on naturalistic queries. Roman Urdu remains the main limitation. That is enough for an honest MS contribution and not enough to claim universal effectiveness.
