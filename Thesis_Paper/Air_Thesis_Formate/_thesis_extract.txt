[82|Normal] Abstract
[83|Normal] Urdu information retrieval (IR) systems face persistent challenges arising from script variability, morphological complexity, and the widespread use of Roman Urdu in user queries, yet existing adaptive retrieval frameworks such as ULTRA rely on a single static, length-based routing threshold that does not adapt to a query's deeper linguistic characteristics. This thesis proposes a dynamic query routing extension to the ULTRA framework, replacing its static threshold with a lightweight, eight-feature Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier combined with a three-tier confidence-based escalation strategy. Queries are additionally processed through an expanded Roman Urdu transliteration dictionary (grown from 30 to 179 entries) to improve retrieval precision on code-mixed and transliterated text. The deployed V2 classifier is trained on 409 labeled Urdu and Roman Urdu queries (193 SHORT, 216 LONG), using a script-aware feature vector (urdu_ratio, roman_ratio, has_urdu, has_roman, query_len, char_len, mixed, urdu_chars). Earlier training and cross-validation experiments showed that this routing task is learnable, reaching 100% routing accuracy (AUC = 1.000) versus 50% for ULTRA's static character-length threshold, with an average routing confidence of 98.18% on a demonstration batch and Roman Urdu retrieval precision (P@15) of 92.5% on queries that the original ULTRA pipeline cannot process (0.00%). Those development-stage figures are not treated as the generalization result. On a later, held-out, frozen 60-query evaluation set (50 primary with gold labels + 10 secondary) that neither the SVM nor the comparison baseline saw during training, the V2 SVM achieved 86.00% accuracy compared with 84.00% for a frozen word-count baseline (word_count >= 6 -> LONG), a small descriptive advantage that is not statistically significant (exact McNemar p = 1.0000; only three discordant pairs). Separately, a Phase 2.5 human retrieval-judgment pilot (33 queries; 330 rank-1-5 judgments) indicates that word count alone is an imperfect proxy for retrieval need, particularly in the 5-word boundary region. Comparison against four LLM-style routing baselines, conducted at the development stage, showed the proposed classifier matching their routing accuracy at approximately three orders of magnitude lower per-query latency (0.4555 ms vs. 600-900 ms) and zero API cost. These results demonstrate that dynamic, confidence-aware query routing is a practical and empirically supported alternative to static routing for Urdu IR, while requiring the frozen held-out evaluation — not the earlier 100% cross-validation figures — to be reported as the headline generalization result.

[203|Heading 2] 1.1 Background
[204|Normal] Urdu is spoken by over 230 million people worldwide and serves as the national language of Pakistan and one of the officially recognized languages of India, yet it remains substantially underserved within contemporary information retrieval (IR) research (Daud et al., 2017). Unlike high-resource languages such as English, where dense retrieval, neural ranking, and large language model (LLM)-based query understanding have matured rapidly — driven by pretrained transformer encoders (Devlin et al., 2019) and dense dual-encoder retrievers (Karpukhin et al., 2020) — Urdu IR continues to face persistent challenges rooted in script complexity, morphological richness, and the informal, code-mixed nature of user-generated queries (Daud et al., 2017; Kazi & Khoja, 2025). A particularly acute challenge is the widespread use of Roman Urdu — Urdu written using the Latin script — in everyday digital communication and search queries, which introduces significant lexical and orthographic variation that conventional retrieval pipelines are not designed to handle (Hussain et al., 2025).
[205|Normal] The trajectory of information retrieval research over the past two decades helps situate why this gap has persisted for so long. Classical retrieval systems relied on lexical matching frameworks such as the probabilistic relevance model underlying BM25 (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009), which score documents based on term-frequency statistics rather than semantic meaning. These frameworks are language-agnostic in principle, but in practice they depend on well-tuned tokenization, stemming, and stop-word resources that were developed first — and most extensively — for English and a handful of other high-resource languages. The subsequent shift toward dense, embedding-based retrieval, exemplified by dual-encoder architectures such as DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020) and pretrained multilingual encoders (Conneau et al., 2020), promised to reduce this dependency by learning semantic representations directly from data. In practice, however, the quality of these representations is itself bounded by the volume and diversity of pretraining data available for a given language, and Urdu — despite its large speaker population — remains comparatively underrepresented in the web-scale corpora used to pretrain most multilingual models.
[206|Normal] This representational gap is compounded by a behavioral one. Empirical observation of Urdu-speaking users' search behavior suggests that a substantial share of real-world queries are not typed in the native Perso-Arabic script at all, but rather transliterated into Roman characters — a consequence of keyboard availability, typing familiarity, and long-standing digital communication habits that predate widespread Urdu-script keyboard support on mobile devices. Sitaram et al. (2019) frame this kind of script alternation as a broader instance of code-switching behavior, a phenomenon that conventional monolingual NLP pipelines, built around the assumption of a single consistent script, are structurally unequipped to handle. The consequence for Urdu information retrieval specifically is that any system evaluated only on native-script queries risks substantially overstating its real-world effectiveness, since it silently excludes a large and linguistically distinct subpopulation of actual user queries.
[207|Normal] The scarcity of standardized evaluation resources compounds this problem. Early efforts such as the Collection for Urdu Retrieval Evaluation (CURE) (Iqbal et al., 2021) and, more recently, the translated Urdu MS MARCO baseline (Butt et al., 2024), have begun to establish benchmarks for Urdu IR, but adaptive, query-aware retrieval strategies for Urdu remain largely unexplored. Where such strategies exist for other languages — for instance, classifier-driven routing between sparse and dense retrieval (Arabzadeh et al., 2021) or complexity-aware retrieval-augmented generation (Jeong et al., 2024) — they have been designed, trained, and validated exclusively on English data, leaving open the question of whether their underlying premise (that a lightweight, query-level classifier can reliably predict the best retrieval strategy) transfers to a morphologically richer, script-variable, lower-resource language such as Urdu.
[208|Normal] The ULTRA framework, proposed by Bashir, Qaiser, and Hussain (2026), represents a recent effort in this direction, introducing a dual-embedding, query-length-sensitive routing scheme that distinguishes between short, intent-oriented queries and longer, context-rich queries using a threshold-based decision process. However, ULTRA's routing mechanism relies on a static query-length threshold, which does not adapt to the full range of linguistic characteristics a real Urdu query may exhibit — including lexical diversity, Roman Urdu transliteration, and question structure. This mirrors a broader challenge identified in the general IR literature: Arabzadeh et al. (2021) showed that a learned classifier for routing queries between sparse and dense retrieval strategies can substantially outperform static rules, though their work was developed and evaluated only for English.
[209|Normal] This thesis extends the ULTRA framework by replacing its static, length-based routing threshold with an adaptive, dynamic query routing mechanism. The proposed system classifies incoming Urdu (and Roman Urdu) queries using a lightweight, eight-feature Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier and routes them to the most appropriate retrieval configuration in real time, combined with a confidence-based tiered escalation strategy for ambiguous queries. In doing so, it treats query routing not as a fixed engineering rule but as a learnable problem in its own right — one that can, and should, be evaluated with the same empirical rigor typically reserved for the retrieval and ranking components of an IR system.
[210|Heading 2] 1.2 Problem Statement
[211|Normal] Existing Urdu information retrieval systems, including the ULTRA framework on which this thesis builds, predominantly employ static, threshold-based routing that applies a fixed decision rule regardless of the deeper linguistic characteristics of a query — such as lexical diversity, script type (native Urdu vs. Roman Urdu), or the presence of interrogative structure. This static approach results in suboptimal retrieval performance for queries that deviate from the assumption the threshold was tuned for, particularly Roman Urdu queries, which suffer from inconsistent transliteration and vocabulary sparsity (Hussain et al., 2025). Preliminary evaluation of static threshold-based routing on a diverse Urdu query set shows accuracy as low as 50%, representing a critical reliability gap for real-world deployment.
[212|Normal] This problem manifests concretely in at least three ways. First, a single scalar threshold on character length cannot separate queries that are short but semantically dense (e.g., a terse but well-formed native-Urdu question) from queries that are short because they are ambiguous or under-specified (e.g., a two-word Roman Urdu fragment). Second, because the static rule has no notion of script type, it applies the identical routing logic to a native-Urdu query and a Roman Urdu query of the same length, despite the two requiring fundamentally different downstream processing — the latter benefiting from transliteration before retrieval is attempted at all. Third, the static threshold offers no mechanism for expressing uncertainty: every query is routed with equal confidence, regardless of how close it sits to the decision boundary, which forecloses the possibility of selectively escalating genuinely ambiguous queries to a more thorough (if more expensive) retrieval strategy while resolving clear-cut queries quickly. Each of these three failure modes is addressed directly by a specific component of the system proposed in this thesis, described fully in Chapter 4.
[213|Heading 2] 1.3 Motivation
[214|Normal] A search system that cannot reliably decide how to handle a query — regardless of how good its underlying retrieval index is — will systematically under-serve a large fraction of its users. This is especially consequential for Urdu, where roughly half of all real-world queries in informal digital settings are transliterated into Roman script rather than written in the native Perso-Arabic script. A routing mechanism that only reasons about raw character length, as in the base ULTRA framework, cannot distinguish between a short but information-dense native Urdu query and a short, ambiguous Roman Urdu fragment. The motivation for this work is therefore twofold: first, to demonstrate that a small set of engineered linguistic features can replace a single scalar threshold and substantially improve routing reliability; and second, to show that this improvement can be delivered without the latency and cost overhead of an LLM-based router, making it practical for real-world, high-throughput Urdu search deployments.
[215|Normal] There is also a broader accessibility dimension to this motivation. Search and information-access tools are increasingly treated as basic digital infrastructure, and a system's usability for a given language community is only as strong as its weakest routing decision, since a single wrong routing choice can mean the difference between a user finding relevant results and abandoning the search entirely. For a language community as large as Urdu speakers, an IR system that only performs well on native-script input effectively excludes a substantial and demographically distinct segment of users — often younger, mobile-first users who default to Roman Urdu out of typing convenience rather than any deficiency in their command of the language. Improving routing reliability for Roman Urdu queries is therefore not merely a technical refinement but a step toward more equitable digital access for Urdu speakers as a whole.
[216|Normal] Finally, from a systems-engineering perspective, the choice between a lightweight, locally-executed classifier and an LLM-based router carries practical consequences that extend beyond raw accuracy. LLM-based routing, of the kind explored for retrieval-augmented generation by Jeong et al. (2024), introduces per-query inference latency and, in most deployment settings, a recurring monetary cost tied to API usage — both of which scale unfavorably with query volume. A classifier that matches LLM-level routing accuracy while running locally, at sub-millisecond latency and no marginal cost, is therefore not just an academic curiosity but a materially more deployable solution for real-world Urdu search infrastructure, particularly in resource-constrained settings where LLM API access may be unreliable, expensive, or unavailable altogether.
[217|Heading 2] 1.4 Research Gap
[218|Normal] Synthesizing the discussion above, the specific research gap this thesis addresses can be stated precisely: no existing Urdu information retrieval system combines (i) a multi-feature, learned query routing mechanism, (ii) explicit, dedicated handling of Roman Urdu transliteration, and (iii) a confidence-based escalation strategy that adapts the retrieval effort applied to a query based on the router's own certainty. Query-adaptive routing has been shown effective for English (Arabzadeh et al., 2021; Jeong et al., 2024), and Urdu-specific IR resources and benchmarks have matured considerably (Iqbal et al., 2021; Butt et al., 2024), but the intersection of these two research threads — learned, confidence-aware routing applied specifically to Urdu and Roman Urdu — has not previously been explored. This gap is examined in full detail, alongside a systematic comparison against twelve individual studies, in the gap analysis presented in Chapter 2 (Section 2.7).
[219|Heading 2] 1.5 Research Questions
[220|Normal] RQ1: Can query-level structural and linguistic features (e.g., character length, lexical diversity, Urdu character ratio, presence of question words) reliably predict the optimal retrieval configuration for a given Urdu query? This question probes whether a small, interpretable set of hand-engineered features is sufficient to separate queries requiring different retrieval strategies, or whether a more complex, learned representation would be necessary — a distinction with direct implications for both routing accuracy and inference latency.
[221|Normal] RQ2: Does a dynamic, machine learning-based query routing mechanism outperform static threshold-based routing, of the kind used in the base ULTRA framework, in terms of routing accuracy and retrieval precision (P@15) across both native Urdu and Roman Urdu queries? This question is the central empirical claim of the thesis. Development-stage comparisons against ULTRA's character-length rule (θ = 150) are reported in Section 5.1; the independent frozen generalization test against a word-count baseline is reported in Section 5.13. The two comparisons answer related but different questions and use different evaluation sets.
[222|Normal] RQ3: Can a confidence-based tiered routing strategy further improve system reliability and efficiency by selectively escalating low-confidence queries to more computationally intensive retrieval methods while resolving high-confidence queries through a lightweight classifier? This question addresses whether calibrated confidence scores, rather than the discrete routing decision alone, carry additional actionable information that can be exploited to make the overall system more efficient without sacrificing accuracy on ambiguous queries.
[223|Heading 2] 1.6 Objectives
[224|List Bullet] Dynamic query classification: Design and train a dynamic SVM-based classifier that predicts optimal query routing decisions using eight structural and linguistic features extracted from Urdu and Roman Urdu queries.
[225|List Bullet] Roman Urdu support: Develop and expand a Roman Urdu transliteration dictionary (from 30 to 179 words) to improve retrieval precision for code-mixed and transliterated queries.
[226|List Bullet] Confidence-based tiered routing: Implement a three-tier confidence routing mechanism balancing retrieval accuracy with computational efficiency.
[227|List Bullet] Comparative evaluation: Benchmark the proposed dynamic routing approach against static threshold-based routing and commercial LLM-based routing methods on the development query sets, and then evaluate the frozen V2 SVM against a frozen word-count baseline on an independent 50-query primary evaluation set (Phase 3B) never used in training or feature verification.
[228|List Bullet] Robustness validation: Statistically validate performance through effect-size analysis, learning-curve evaluation, regularization sensitivity, diagnostic held-out checks, a label-audit before/after comparison (Stage-C McNemar), and the frozen Phase 3B McNemar test against the word-count baseline.
[229|Normal] These five objectives were sequenced deliberately: the first two (classifier design and Roman Urdu support) establish the core technical contribution, the third (tiered routing) builds a practical deployment layer on top of that contribution, and the final two (comparative evaluation and robustness validation) ensure that any observed improvement is both meaningful relative to competing approaches and statistically defensible rather than an artifact of a single favorable train-test split.

[237|Normal] Contributions 1 through 3 are architectural: together, they constitute the dynamic routing system itself, evaluated end-to-end in Chapter 5. Contribution 4 is resource-oriented, extending a component (the transliteration dictionary) that is reusable independently of the classifier and could, in principle, benefit other Urdu NLP systems beyond retrieval routing. Contributions 5 and 6 are evaluative: the comparative benchmark situates the proposed system relative to both the simplest possible baseline (static thresholding) and the most sophisticated plausible alternative (LLM-based routing), while the ablation study provides evidence for which architectural choices are load-bearing and which are comparatively redundant — a distinction of practical value for anyone seeking to reproduce or extend this work.
[238|Heading 2] 1.8 Scope of the Thesis
[239|Normal] This thesis is scoped to the query-routing layer of an Urdu information retrieval pipeline. It focuses on (i) the design of an eight-feature query representation and a Support Vector Machine classifier for routing decisions, (ii) a confidence-based tiered escalation strategy, and (iii) a Roman Urdu transliteration module that extends coverage to code-mixed queries. The retrieval backend itself (a ChromaDB vector store over a single news-domain corpus of 111,860 Urdu articles) is treated as fixed infrastructure inherited from the base ULTRA framework; this thesis does not propose a new embedding model or a new document ranking function. Evaluation is restricted to a news-domain corpus and to the query sets described in Chapter 4; generalization to other domains (e.g., legal or medical Urdu text) is discussed as future work in Chapter 6.
[240|Normal] Several adjacent problems are deliberately excluded from this scope so that the routing contribution can be evaluated in isolation, without confounding it with improvements to unrelated pipeline components. Document-level embedding quality, corpus preprocessing, and index construction are inherited unchanged from the base ULTRA framework and are not re-optimized here. Similarly, this thesis does not attempt to improve the underlying sentence embedding model, nor does it explore alternative vector index structures beyond the HNSW configuration already in use; any accuracy or precision gains reported in Chapter 5 are therefore attributable specifically to the routing layer, not to incidental improvements elsewhere in the pipeline. Query understanding beyond the eight engineered features — for instance, named entity recognition or syntactic parsing of the query — is likewise outside the scope of this work, though it is discussed as a possible direction for future extension in Chapter 6.
[241|Heading 2] 1.9 Significance of the Study
[242|Normal] The significance of this study is best understood at three levels. At the technical level, it demonstrates that a small set of interpretable, cheaply computed query features, combined with a classical machine learning classifier, can match the routing accuracy of substantially more expensive LLM-based alternatives — a finding with implications for query routing research beyond Urdu specifically, since it suggests that engineered features remain competitive with LLM-based approaches for well-scoped classification tasks even in the current era of large pretrained models. At the linguistic and regional level, this work contributes one of the few dedicated treatments of Roman Urdu within a retrieval (rather than sentiment or offensive-language classification) context, extending the applicability of Urdu IR research to the code-mixed, informally written queries that plausibly constitute a large share of real-world search traffic from Urdu-speaking users. At the practical level, the efficiency profile of the proposed classifier — sub-millisecond inference at zero marginal cost — makes the findings directly actionable for practitioners building Urdu-language search or recommendation systems who must operate under real-world latency and cost constraints that LLM-based routing would violate.

[250|Heading 1] Chapter 2: Literature Survey
[251|Normal] Urdu information retrieval sits at the intersection of three research threads that are individually well studied for high-resource languages but remain fragmented for Urdu: general Urdu natural language processing, information retrieval benchmarking, and query-adaptive routing. This chapter reviews representative work in each thread, situates the proposed approach within the broader progression from lexical to neural to LLM-based retrieval methods, surveys the datasets and evaluation metrics relevant to this progression, and identifies the specific gap this thesis addresses.
[252|Heading 2] 2.1 Foundations
[253|Heading 3] 2.1.1 Urdu Natural Language Processing
[254|Normal] Daud et al. (2017) conducted a comprehensive survey of Urdu language processing, cataloguing the linguistic characteristics that complicate computational treatment of the language: a cursive, context-sensitive Perso-Arabic script written right-to-left, rich morphology, and the absence of large-scale standardized corpora compared to English or Arabic. Their survey remains a foundational reference for understanding why Urdu NLP tools lag behind those available for high-resource languages, though it predates the transformer era and therefore does not address embedding-based retrieval or query-adaptive systems.
[255|Normal] Kazi and Khoja (2025) more recently proposed a framework for Urdu language document retrieval, applying deep learning techniques and evaluating the effect of different word embeddings on retrieval-adjacent tasks such as named entity recognition. Their framework focuses on document-level representation quality rather than on how individual queries should be routed to different retrieval strategies based on their structural characteristics.
[256|Normal] A recurring theme across the Urdu NLP literature more broadly is that morphological and orthographic complexity does not merely add noise to a pipeline built for English — it changes which pipeline design choices are even viable. Techniques that work well for space-delimited, Latin-script languages, such as simple whitespace tokenization or suffix-stripping stemmers, transfer poorly to a cursive, context-sensitive script in which letter shape depends on position within a word. This has practical consequences for query routing specifically: a feature such as word_count, straightforward to compute for English, requires a tokenization decision for Urdu that is itself non-trivial, and the eight-feature representation adopted in this thesis (Chapter 3) was designed with this constraint in mind, favoring features that remain well-defined under simple whitespace tokenization rather than relying on a full morphological analyzer that does not yet exist at production quality for Urdu.
[257|Heading 3] 2.1.2 Roman Urdu Processing Challenges
[258|Normal] Roman Urdu — Urdu transliterated into the Latin script — lacks a standardized spelling convention, meaning the same word may be written in multiple ways depending on the writer's phonetic intuition. Hussain et al. (2025) addressed a related problem, fine-tuning large language models via QLoRA for offensive language detection in Roman Urdu-English code-mixed text, and explicitly identified inconsistent spelling, unstated grammar, and scarcity of labeled data as core obstacles for any Roman Urdu NLP task. Their work targets classification rather than retrieval, and does not address how Roman Urdu queries should be routed differently from native-script Urdu queries within an IR pipeline.
[259|Normal] More broadly, Sitaram et al. (2019) surveyed code-switched speech and language processing, characterizing the alternation between languages and scripts — of which Roman Urdu is a script-level instance — as a communicative phenomenon that conventional monolingual NLP pipelines are not designed to handle. Within Urdu specifically, Mehmood et al. (2020) tackled the closely related task of Roman Urdu sentiment analysis, proposing a hybrid multi-channel architecture over custom Word2Vec, FastText, and GloVe embeddings and reporting F1-score gains of up to 9% over adapted machine-learning baselines. Both works confirm that Roman Urdu requires script-aware, dedicated processing rather than being treated as a minor variant of native-script Urdu, but neither addresses query routing or retrieval.
[260|Normal] Taken together, this body of work suggests two broadly viable strategies for handling Roman Urdu within an NLP pipeline: (i) a dictionary- or rule-based transliteration approach that maps Roman Urdu tokens to their native-script equivalents before downstream processing, as adopted in this thesis, or (ii) an end-to-end learned approach in which a model is trained directly on Roman Urdu text without explicit transliteration, as in Hussain et al.'s (2025) QLoRA fine-tuning and Mehmood et al.'s (2020) custom embedding approach. The dictionary-based strategy was preferred here for two practical reasons: it requires no additional model training or labeled Roman Urdu corpus beyond the dictionary itself, and it produces a transliterated query that can be fed directly into the same native-Urdu embedding pipeline already used for the retrieval backend, avoiding the need to maintain two separate embedding spaces.
[261|Heading 3] 2.1.3 Low-Resource and Cross-Lingual Retrieval Limitations
[262|Normal] Urdu's challenges are not unique; a growing body of work shows that state-of-the-art dense retrieval degrades sharply once a language falls outside the small set of high-resource languages that dominate pretraining corpora. Wu et al. (2024) evaluated the multilingual Dense Passage Retriever (mDPR) — the retrieval component of the Cross-lingual Open-Retrieval Answer Generation (CORA) pipeline — on two extremely low-resource languages, Amharic and Khmer, and found that even after targeted extensions using translation language modelling and question-passage alignment, retrieval quality improvements were modest and absolute performance remained low. Their finding that language alignment techniques only partially close the gap for low-resource languages is directly relevant to Urdu: general-purpose multilingual dense retrievers cannot be assumed to transfer well without language-specific adaptation.
[263|Normal] Closer to the transliteration problem this thesis addresses, Chari et al. (2025) observed that current search systems are not usually trained on low-resource language varieties, forcing users to express queries in a high-resource language instead — a pattern directly analogous to Urdu speakers defaulting to Roman (Latin-script) transliteration when native-script input is inconvenient. They proposed fine-tuning neural rankers on pairs of related language varieties so that the ranker learns to exploit shared lexical and grammatical structure, improving retrieval effectiveness for the trained varieties.
[264|Normal] Conneau et al. (2020) showed that scaling multilingual pretraining (XLM-R) improves Urdu XNLI accuracy by 11.4 points over prior multilingual models, and that low-resource languages such as Urdu benefit disproportionately from scale — yet this gain is realized at the level of a single monolithic embedding model applied uniformly to every query, with no mechanism for detecting when a given query's script, length, or lexical structure would be better served by a different retrieval strategy altogether. Together, these studies confirm a consistent pattern across unrelated low-resource settings: general-purpose neural retrieval degrades for under-represented languages and scripts, and closing the gap requires language- or script-specific engineering rather than relying on multilingual pretraining alone. This motivates the explicit Roman Urdu detection and dictionary-transliteration module used in this thesis's methodology.
[265|Heading 3] 2.1.4 Information Retrieval Benchmarking for Urdu
[266|Normal] Iqbal et al. (2021) introduced CURE (Collection for Urdu Retrieval Evaluation), a standard test collection of Urdu documents built specifically to enable rigorous IR evaluation, addressing the absence of a shared benchmark comparable to TREC-style collections available for English. CURE provides an evaluation resource but does not itself propose an adaptive retrieval or routing mechanism.
[267|Normal] Butt et al. (2024) established the first large-scale Urdu IR dataset by machine-translating the MS MARCO passage ranking dataset into Urdu, and benchmarked both BM25 and fine-tuned multilingual transformer (mT5-based mMARCO) baselines, reporting a Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR@10) of 0.247 for their best fine-tuned configuration. Their evaluation applies a single retrieval strategy uniformly to all queries; no mechanism is proposed for detecting which queries would benefit from a different retrieval configuration.
[268|Heading 2] 2.2 Comparative Analysis of Retrieval and Routing Paradigms
[269|Normal] The methods surveyed in Section 2.1 and Section 2.6 span three broad technical paradigms — classical statistical/lexical methods, classical machine learning classifiers, and deep learning or LLM-based approaches — each carrying distinct trade-offs relevant to the query-routing problem addressed in this thesis. This section compares these paradigms explicitly, since the choice of an RBF-kernel SVM for the proposed routing classifier (rather than a lexical rule, a deep neural classifier, or an LLM prompt) was made deliberately in light of these trade-offs, rather than as a default choice.
[270|Heading 3] 2.2.1 Lexical and Statistical Methods
[271|Normal] The earliest and still most widely deployed retrieval paradigm relies on lexical term-matching, exemplified by the BM25 ranking function (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009), which scores document relevance from term-frequency and inverse-document-frequency statistics without requiring any learned representation. Applied to query routing rather than document ranking, an equivalent lexical strategy would route queries using simple surface statistics — precisely what the static, character-length threshold in the base ULTRA framework does. This paradigm's principal advantage is transparency and near-zero computational cost; its principal limitation, as demonstrated empirically in Chapter 5, is that surface statistics alone are insufficient to separate queries whose true routing requirement depends on script type or lexical structure rather than length alone.
[272|Heading 3] 2.2.2 Classical Machine Learning Approaches
[273|Normal] Between purely lexical rules and deep neural networks lies a family of classical machine learning classifiers — logistic regression, decision trees, and kernel methods such as the Support Vector Machine (Cortes & Vapnik, 1995) — that learn a decision boundary over a fixed, hand-engineered feature representation. This is the paradigm adopted in this thesis: an RBF-kernel SVM trained over the eight-dimensional feature vector described in Chapter 3. The appeal of this family, relative to both lexical rules and deep learning, is threefold: it requires substantially less training data than a deep neural classifier (the 409-query training set used here would likely be insufficient to train a competitive neural classifier from scratch), it produces calibratable confidence scores via Platt scaling without requiring a separate calibration architecture, and it executes at sub-millisecond inference latency on commodity hardware, without requiring a GPU.
[274|Heading 3] 2.2.3 Deep Learning and Neural Ranking Approaches
[275|Normal] Deep learning approaches to retrieval and classification, typically built on pretrained transformer encoders such as BERT (Devlin et al., 2019) and its multilingual variants (Conneau et al., 2020), learn representations directly from raw text rather than from hand-engineered features. Arabzadeh et al. (2021), for instance, trained a BERT-based classifier for sparse-versus-dense retrieval routing, and dense retrieval itself — as in DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020) and Sentence-BERT (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) — relies on transformer encoders to produce the embeddings compared during retrieval. The retrieval backend inherited from the base ULTRA framework in this thesis already uses this paradigm (via paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 embeddings, Section 4.2). For the routing decision specifically, however, a full transformer-based classifier was judged disproportionate to the task: routing is a comparatively low-dimensional decision (in this thesis, effectively binary, short-vs-long, augmented with confidence tiering) relative to the open-ended semantic matching that dense retrieval must perform, and the accuracy ceiling on this routing task (Chapter 5) is reached by the much lighter SVM classifier without requiring transformer-scale representations.
[276|Heading 3] 2.2.4 Large Language Model-Based Approaches
[277|Normal] The most recent paradigm applies large language models directly to routing and retrieval-adjacent decisions through prompting, rather than through a separately trained classifier. Jeong et al.'s (2024) Adaptive-RAG and Hsu and Tzeng's (2025) DAT both fall into this category, using an LLM to assess query complexity or to calibrate retrieval fusion weights at inference time. This paradigm's chief advantage is flexibility: it requires no dedicated training data or model training step, since the LLM's general-purpose reasoning is applied directly to the routing decision through a prompt. Its chief disadvantages, quantified directly in this thesis's evaluation (Chapter 5, Section 5.3), are per-query inference latency (hundreds of milliseconds, versus sub-millisecond for a local classifier) and, in most deployment settings, a recurring monetary cost tied to API usage that scales with query volume — both of which are avoided entirely by the SVM-based approach adopted here.
[278|Caption] Table 1. Comparative summary of retrieval/routing paradigms across cost and capability dimensions.
[279|Normal] This comparison motivates the specific design choice defended empirically in Chapter 5: the classical machine learning paradigm (Section 2.2.2) occupies a favorable position on the cost-capability trade-off for the query-routing task specifically — substantially more capable than lexical thresholding, while avoiding the training-data and inference-cost overhead of deep learning and LLM-based alternatives.

[284|Heading 2] 2.4 Evaluation Metrics in Information Retrieval
[285|Normal] Evaluating an IR system with a routing component requires metrics at two distinct levels: routing-decision quality and retrieval-result quality. This thesis reports both, and this section briefly surveys the metrics used and their provenance in the literature.
[286|Normal] At the routing-decision level, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score (Equations 3.8-3.11) are standard classification metrics applicable whenever routing is framed as a discrete decision, as it is in this thesis and in Arabzadeh et al.'s (2021) sparse-versus-dense classifier. Because the SVM classifier additionally produces calibrated confidence scores via Platt scaling, this thesis also reports the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and its Area Under the Curve (AUC), a threshold-independent measure of separability between classes; the importance of properly calibrated (rather than merely high) confidence scores for this kind of reporting is discussed at length by Guo et al. (2017), whose finding that modern classifiers tend toward systematic overconfidence directly motivated the use of Platt scaling (Section 3.4) rather than reporting raw decision-function margins as confidence.
[287|Normal] At the retrieval-result level, Precision at rank k (P@k) — used here at k=15, following the top-15 retrieval depth of the underlying ChromaDB query — measures the proportion of returned results that are relevant, and is the metric used throughout Chapter 5 to compare retrieval quality between the static and proposed routing mechanisms. Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR), used by Butt et al. (2024) to benchmark Urdu MS MARCO, instead measures how early the first relevant result appears in the ranked list, and is more appropriate when a query is understood to have a single primary relevant answer rather than a broader set of relevant documents, as in Urdu news retrieval. P@15 was preferred over MRR in this thesis because the underlying retrieval task — surfacing a topically relevant set of news articles — more closely resembles a multi-relevant-document retrieval setting than a single-answer question-answering setting.
[288|Normal] Finally, because a central claim of this thesis concerns deployment practicality relative to LLM-based routing (Section 2.2.4), two efficiency metrics not traditionally reported in retrieval-accuracy-focused IR literature are also measured directly in Chapter 5: per-query inference latency and per-query monetary cost. These are not standard IR metrics in the way P@k or MRR are, but they are essential to substantiating the practical, rather than purely academic, motivation for this thesis (Section 1.3).
[289|Heading 2] 2.5 Current Research Trends
[290|Normal] Three trends visible across the literature reviewed in this chapter are worth drawing out explicitly, since they collectively motivate the specific architecture proposed in this thesis. First, retrieval-augmented generation has emerged as the dominant paradigm for combining parametric language models with non-parametric retrieval (Lewis et al., 2020), and a growing sub-thread within this paradigm — adaptive or complexity-aware RAG (Jeong et al., 2024) and dynamic hybrid-fusion weighting (Hsu & Tzeng, 2025) — has moved away from applying a single fixed retrieval strategy to every query, toward query-dependent strategy selection. The three-tier confidence routing architecture proposed in this thesis is a direct instance of this broader trend, applied for the first time, to the authors' knowledge, to Urdu.
[291|Normal] Second, the multilingual NLP literature has increasingly recognized that scaling pretraining data and model size (Conneau et al., 2020) helps but does not fully resolve the performance gap for low-resource languages and scripts, motivating a parallel trend toward targeted, language-specific engineering — explicit transliteration modules, curated dictionaries, and script-detection components — rather than relying solely on ever-larger multilingual models (Chari et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024). The Roman Urdu transliteration module developed in this thesis (Section 4.3) is positioned within this second trend.
[292|Normal] Third, and more broadly across machine learning, there is growing scrutiny of the assumption that larger, more general models are unconditionally preferable to smaller, task-specific ones, particularly once deployment cost and latency are accounted for alongside raw accuracy. The direct efficiency comparison conducted in this thesis (Chapter 5, Section 5.3) between a lightweight SVM classifier and LLM-based routing baselines contributes an Urdu-specific, routing-specific data point to this broader ongoing discussion.
[293|Heading 2] 2.6 Prior Work
[294|Normal] Outside the Urdu-specific literature, query routing between retrieval strategies has been studied more extensively for English. The underlying premise — that queries differ systematically in how difficult they are to satisfy, and that this difficulty can be estimated before or during retrieval — was formalized early by Carmel and Yom-Tov (2010) in their synthesis of query performance prediction (QPP) methods, which surveyed pre- and post-retrieval signals for anticipating when a query is likely to fail. Classical QPP estimates how well a fixed retrieval strategy will perform on a given query, but does not itself select among alternative retrieval strategies or scripts, and was not developed with Urdu or code-mixed queries in mind.
[295|Normal] Arabzadeh et al. (2021) proposed a classifier that selects between sparse (BM25), dense, and hybrid retrieval strategies on a per-query basis, training a BERT-based model to predict which strategy would be most effective for a given query before retrieval is performed. Their approach reduced computational cost while maintaining retrieval effectiveness, by routing “easy” queries to the cheaper sparse retriever and reserving the dense retriever for queries it is more likely to help. This routing framework was designed and evaluated exclusively on English MS MARCO data; it does not address Urdu script variation, Roman Urdu transliteration, or confidence-tiered escalation.
[296|Normal] Building on the retrieval-augmented generation paradigm introduced by Lewis et al. (2020) — in which a parametric generator is conditioned on passages retrieved from a non-parametric index rather than relying on retrieval alone — Jeong et al. (2024) proposed Adaptive-RAG, a framework in which a lightweight classifier, trained on automatically collected complexity labels, predicts how complex an incoming question is and routes it to one of three retrieval-augmented generation strategies: no retrieval for the simplest queries, single-step retrieval for moderately complex queries, and iterative multi-step retrieval for the most complex queries. This three-way, classifier-driven escalation strategy is structurally analogous to the three-tier confidence routing mechanism proposed in this thesis, though Adaptive-RAG operationalizes complexity as a property of the question's reasoning depth rather than a calibrated classifier confidence score, and its classifier is a fine-tuned language model rather than a lightweight, low-latency SVM.
[297|Normal] Also relevant to the hybrid-search tier of this thesis's routing design, Hsu and Tzeng (2025) proposed DAT (Dynamic Alpha Tuning), a hybrid retrieval framework that abandons static dense/sparse fusion weights in favor of a per-query weighting factor, computed by having a large language model score the effectiveness of each retriever's top candidate before calibrating the fusion weight accordingly. Their central finding — that fixed hybrid-fusion weights are consistently suboptimal — provides independent support for the design choice, in this thesis's MEDIUM-confidence tier, of invoking hybrid search only adaptively rather than as a fixed default strategy. However, their LLM-based scoring adds non-trivial latency and cost per query, which is impractical at the throughput this thesis targets.
[298|Normal] Most directly relevant to this thesis, Bashir, Qaiser, and Hussain (2026) proposed ULTRA, a dual-embedding Urdu recommendation architecture with a query-length-sensitive routing scheme: queries are routed to either a title/headline-level or a full-content/document-level semantic pipeline based on a length threshold, reporting precision gains above 90% over single-pipeline baselines on a large-scale Urdu news corpus. ULTRA's routing decision is based on a single static, length-based threshold: it does not incorporate lexical diversity, Urdu-character ratio, question-word presence, or Roman Urdu detection as routing signals, and provides no confidence-based mechanism for escalating ambiguous queries. This is precisely the gap this thesis addresses.

[300|Normal] The table below summarizes the research gaps identified above, positioning this thesis relative to existing work. Collectively, the reviewed literature reveals three converging gaps that, individually, have each been addressed in isolation but never jointly, and never for Urdu. First, Urdu-specific NLP and IR resources have matured considerably, but remain either pre-neural in scope or focused on document-level representation and benchmarking rather than query-level adaptivity. Second, work on low-resource and cross-lingual retrieval confirms that general-purpose multilingual embeddings degrade for under-represented languages and scripts, motivating explicit, language-specific engineering. Third, query-adaptive routing has been repeatedly shown effective in high-resource-language IR — yet every one of these systems is evaluated exclusively on English and offers no mechanism for script-mixed or transliterated queries. Beyond stating each equation, this chapter also motivates the specific mathematical choices made — why an RBF kernel rather than a linear kernel, why Platt scaling rather than an alternative calibration method, and why the particular confidence thresholds (85% and 60%) were selected — and analyzes the computational complexity of the resulting pipeline, since deployment efficiency is a central claim of this thesis (Section 1.3).
[306|Heading 2] 3.1 Query Feature Vector Representation
[307|Normal] Each query q is converted into an eight-dimensional feature vector
[308|Normal] x(q) = [x₁(q), x₂(q), …, x₈(q)] ∈ ℝ⁸
[309|Normal] (3.1)
[310|Normal] combining script-composition, length, and character-count signals, as defined in Table 3.1. This is the canonical V2 vector persisted in models/training_info.json and verified in Phase 3A. Unlike an earlier development feature set, V2 does not include is_long_by_static, lexical_diversity, or has_question_words; script-aware ratios replace those signals. Feature x₇ (mixed) is structurally available but was unused in training (no mixed-script examples; scaler mean 0 and scale 1 on that dimension).

[312|Heading 3] 3.1.1 Rationale for Feature Selection
[313|Normal] The eight V2 features in Table 3.1 were chosen to remain computable in a single pass over the query, without a tagger, parser, or extra model call. x₁-x₄ encode script composition (Urdu-character ratio, dictionary-based Roman ratio, and the corresponding binary flags), directly addressing the native-versus-Roman distinction in Section 1.2. x₅-x₆ encode length in words and characters, generalizing ULTRA's single scalar. x₇ flags mixed-script queries for future coverage; it is currently a dead feature. x₈ counts Urdu characters in absolute terms, complementing the ratio. An earlier development set used lexical diversity and question-word flags; those remain historically informative (Section 5.4) but are not inputs to the frozen V2 model.
[314|Heading 2] 3.2 Feature Standardization
[315|Normal] The eight-dimensional feature vector x(q) is standardized using a fitted feature scaler,
[316|Normal] z(q) = (x(q) − μ) / σ
[317|Normal] (3.2)
[318|Normal] Standardization is not a cosmetic step: the eight V2 raw features span markedly different numeric ranges (ratios, binaries, word count, character length, and an absolute Urdu-character count). An RBF kernel's distance computation (Section 3.3) is sensitive to relative scale. Without standardization, char_len and urdu_chars would dominate the kernel purely as an artifact of numeric range, drowning out ratio and binary features regardless of their true discriminative value. The fitted scaler is the deployed models/scaler.pkl object verified in Phase 3A.
[319|Heading 2] 3.3 SVM Decision Function
[320|Normal] The standardized vector z(q) is passed to a trained Support Vector Machine classifier (Cortes & Vapnik, 1995), whose decision function takes the general kernelized form
[321|Normal] f(z(q)) = Σᵢ αᵢ yᵢ K(z(q), zᵢ) + b
[322|Normal] (3.3)
[323|Normal] where zᵢ are the support vectors identified during training, αᵢ their learned dual coefficients, yᵢ their class labels, b the learned bias term, and K(·,·) the kernel function. The classifier uses a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel with probability estimates enabled via Platt scaling, and a fixed random seed (random_state = 42) for reproducibility. Regularization (C) and kernel coefficient (γ) are left at scikit-learn defaults (C = 1.0, γ = 'scale'). The predicted routing class is
[324|Normal] ŷ(q) = sign( f(z(q)) )
[325|Normal] (3.4)
[326|Normal] for the binary case, generalized to arg-max over class scores for the multi-class setting.
[327|Heading 3] 3.3.1 Why an RBF Kernel
[328|Normal] Three kernel choices were considered for the SVM: linear, polynomial, and RBF. A linear kernel restricts the decision boundary to a hyperplane in the original eight-dimensional feature space, appropriate only if SHORT/LONG are linearly separable there — a strong assumption given that V2 mixes ratios, binaries, and raw counts. A polynomial kernel adds a degree hyperparameter prone to overfitting on 409 training queries. The RBF kernel captures non-linear boundaries without that degree parameter. Gamma was left at scikit-learn's 'scale' default. Development-stage accuracy is robust across a wide C range (Section 5.7); generalization is assessed separately on frozen Phase 3B (86.00%).
[329|Heading 2] 3.4 Confidence Calibration
[330|Normal] Beyond the discrete class label, the classifier produces a calibrated confidence score by applying a sigmoid (Platt scaling) transformation to the decision function output:
[331|Normal] C(q) = 1 / (1 + e⁻⁽ᴬ·ᶠ⁽ᶻ⁽ᵢ⁾⁾⁺ᴮ⁾) × 100%
[332|Normal] (3.5)
[333|Normal] where A and B are calibration parameters fitted during training, and C(q) ∈ [0, 100]% is the confidence score used to drive the tiered routing decision. Platt scaling was chosen because it directly fits the confidence estimate to hold-out label agreement rather than deriving it heuristically from the decision margin alone, which mitigates the general tendency of modern classifiers toward overconfidence (Guo et al., 2017).
[334|Normal] An alternative to Platt scaling considered during design was isotonic regression, a non-parametric calibration method that can, in principle, fit a more flexible calibration curve than the two-parameter sigmoid Platt scaling assumes. Isotonic regression was not adopted here for a practical reason specific to this thesis's dataset scale: it is a non-parametric method with a larger effective number of degrees of freedom, and is known to be more prone to overfitting the calibration curve itself when the calibration set is small — a meaningful risk given that even the 409-query training set leaves a comparatively small portion leaves a comparatively small held-out portion for calibration. Platt scaling's two-parameter sigmoid form is more conservative under these data constraints, at the cost of assuming the calibration curve is well-approximated by a sigmoid, an assumption the near-perfect separability of the routing task (Chapter 5, ROC-AUC = 1.000) makes relatively benign in this specific application.
[335|Heading 2] 3.5 Confidence-Based Three-Tier Routing Function
[336|Normal] The routing confidence score C(q) determines one of three adaptive search behaviors through the piecewise routing function
[337|Normal] R(q) = Full Semantic Search,  if C(q) ≥ 85%  (HIGH)
[338|Normal] R(q) = Hybrid Search,  if 60% ≤ C(q) < 85%  (MEDIUM)
[339|Normal] R(q) = Query Expansion,  if C(q) < 60%  (LOW)
[340|Normal] (3.6)
[341|Normal] The specific thresholds 85% and 60% were chosen to reflect two qualitatively different levels of risk tolerance rather than being fitted directly to the evaluation data (which would risk overstating the routing function's generalization). The 85% HIGH threshold reflects a conservative bar: below it, even a classifier with strong average accuracy could plausibly be wrong often enough to justify the additional retrieval effort of hybrid search. The 60% LOW threshold marks the point below which the classifier's confidence is close enough to the 50% decision boundary that its prediction is only marginally better than chance, at which point query expansion — broadening rather than narrowing the search — is the more conservative retrieval strategy. Because these thresholds are fixed constants rather than data-fitted parameters, they can be adjusted post-deployment (e.g., raising the HIGH threshold to 90% to further favor hybrid search) without retraining the underlying SVM classifier, decoupling the routing policy from the classification model.
[342|Caption] Algorithm 3.1. Dynamic Query Routing (end-to-end).
[344|Heading 2] 3.6 Retrieval Scoring Function
[345|Normal] All three routing tiers ultimately query the same underlying retrieval backend: a ChromaDB vector store built on the 111,860-article corpus, using an HNSW index with cosine similarity. Given a query embedding e_q and a candidate document embedding e_d (both produced by paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2), relevance is scored as
[346|Normal] sim(e_q, e_d) = (e_q · e_d) / (‖e_q‖ × ‖e_d‖)
[347|Normal] (3.7)
[348|Normal] and the top-15 documents by sim(e_q, e_d) are returned. The HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) index approximates this nearest-neighbor search sub-linearly, avoiding an exhaustive comparison against all 111,860 article embeddings for every query. Cosine similarity, rather than Euclidean distance, was retained from the base ULTRA framework because it normalizes away magnitude differences between embedding vectors, focusing the similarity score purely on the angular (directional) alignment between query and document representations — a property generally preferred for sentence-embedding-based retrieval, where the raw magnitude of an embedding vector is not itself a meaningful signal of relevance.
[349|Heading 2] 3.7 Evaluation Metrics
[350|Normal] The system is evaluated along two complementary dimensions: routing accuracy and retrieval relevance. For the routing task, standard binary-classification outcomes are defined relative to the routing decision, from which routing accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score are computed:
[351|Normal] Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + FP + FN + TN)
[352|Normal] (3.8)
[353|Normal] Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
[354|Normal] (3.9)
[355|Normal] Recall = TP / (TP + FN)
[356|Normal] (3.10)
[357|Normal] F1 = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall)
[358|Normal] (3.11)
[359|Normal] For retrieval quality, Precision at rank 15 (P@15) is used:
[360|Normal] P@15 = (Relevant documents in top-15 results) / 15
[361|Normal] (3.12)
[362|Normal] The routing classifier's average confidence across the evaluation set is additionally reported as
[363|Normal] C̄ = (1/N) Σᵢ C(qᵢ)
[364|Normal] (3.13)
[365|Normal] where N is the number of evaluation queries and C(qᵢ) is the per-query confidence score from Equation 3.5.
[366|Heading 2] 3.8 Computational Complexity Analysis
[367|Normal] Because deployment efficiency relative to LLM-based routing is a central claim of this thesis, it is useful to state the computational complexity of each pipeline stage explicitly. Feature extraction (Section 3.1) is O(|q|) in the length of the query string, since every one of the eight features can be computed with a single pass over the query's characters or whitespace-delimited tokens. Feature standardization (Eq. 3.2) is O(1) per feature, hence O(8) = O(1) overall, since it is a fixed elementwise affine transform. SVM inference (Eq. 3.3) is O(n_sv × d) where n_sv is the number of support vectors retained after training and d = 8 is the feature dimensionality; because n_sv is fixed at training time and does not grow with corpus size, this step's cost is constant with respect to the size of the underlying document corpus. The retrieval step itself (Eq. 3.7) is the only stage whose cost depends on corpus size, and even there the HNSW index reduces the expected query complexity from the O(|Corpus|) of an exhaustive nearest-neighbor search to approximately O(log|Corpus|) under typical HNSW graph-construction parameters. Table 3.2 summarizes these complexity bounds alongside the measured wall-clock latency reported in Chapter 5.

[374|Heading 1] Chapter 4: Methods Developed
[375|Normal] This chapter describes the methodology developed to replace the static, length-based query routing mechanism of the base ULTRA framework (Bashir et al., 2026) — a fixed character-length threshold of θ = 150 — with a dynamic, learned routing architecture. The system combines Roman Urdu detection and transliteration, an eight-feature semantic classifier, a confidence-calibrated Support Vector Machine, and a three-tier adaptive search strategy layered on top of a dense retrieval backend. Each section below pairs a description of what was built with the specific design decisions and parameter choices made during implementation, so that the methodology is reproducible rather than stated only at a conceptual level.
[376|Heading 2] 4.1 System Architecture
[377|Normal] The system architecture (Figure 4.1) illustrates the end-to-end pipeline, organized into four sequential stages: (i) language-variant normalization, (ii) feature-based query representation, (iii) confidence-calibrated routing, and (iv) tiered retrieval execution. Each stage is designed to be independently replaceable, so that the routing signal (Stages ii-iii) can evolve without requiring changes to the underlying retrieval backend (Stage iv).
[378|Normal] Stage 1 — Language-variant normalization. An incoming query q is first passed through a Roman Urdu detection module. If q is identified as Roman Urdu, it is transliterated using the expanded 179-word dictionary before proceeding to Stage 2; native-script Urdu queries bypass transliteration and pass through unchanged.
[379|Normal] Stage 2 — Feature-based query representation. The (possibly transliterated) query is converted into an eight-dimensional feature vector x(q) (Table 3.1) capturing character-level, word-level, lexical-diversity, and script-composition signals. This replaces ULTRA's single scalar feature (raw character length) with a structured, multi-dimensional representation.
[380|Normal] Stage 3 — Confidence-calibrated routing. x(q) is standardized using a fitted feature scaler and passed to the trained SVM classifier, which outputs both a routing decision ŷ and a calibrated confidence score C(q) ∈ [0, 100]%.
[381|Normal] Stage 4 — Tiered retrieval execution. C(q) is passed through the three-tier routing function (Equation 3.6), which selects one of three retrieval strategies — full semantic search, hybrid search, or query expansion — depending on whether C(q) falls in the HIGH, MEDIUM, or LOW confidence band. Regardless of which tier is selected, the chosen strategy ultimately issues a similarity query against the same ChromaDB vector store to return the top-15 relevant articles.
[383|Caption] Figure 1. System architecture: end-to-end dynamic query routing pipeline.
[384|Normal] Reading Figure 4.1 left to right traces the lifecycle of a single query through the system: the diagram's leftmost block represents the raw query as typed by a user, and each subsequent block corresponds to exactly one of the four stages described above, with the arrows between blocks representing the data passed forward — first the (possibly transliterated) query string, then the eight-dimensional feature vector, then the routing decision and confidence score, and finally the retrieved document set. The branching point after Stage 3 is the diagram's most important structural feature: it is the only point in the pipeline where the flow of control, rather than merely the data, depends on a runtime value (the confidence score C(q)), and it is this branching point that operationalizes the three-tier routing function formalized in Equation 3.6.

[389|Heading 2] 4.2 Corpus and Dataset
[390|Normal] The retrieval backend is built over a corpus of 111,860 Urdu news articles (Kaggle Urdu News Dataset), embedded as 384-dimensional vectors using the paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 sentence embedding model — a multilingual instance of the Sentence-BERT family (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) — and indexed in ChromaDB using an HNSW index with cosine similarity search.
[391|Heading 3] 4.2.1 Corpus Preprocessing
[392|Normal] Prior to embedding, the raw corpus is passed through a preprocessing stage inherited from the base ULTRA framework, consisting of four sequential steps: (i) removal of HTML artifacts and non-textual boilerplate (bylines, publication timestamps, and navigation text) that would otherwise pollute the embedding signal; (ii) de-duplication of near-identical articles, which are common in news corpora due to wire-service syndication of the same story across multiple outlets; (iii) category normalization, mapping inconsistent or overlapping category labels in the raw dataset onto the four consolidated categories reported in Table 4.2; and (iv) minimum-length filtering, discarding articles too short to carry meaningful topical content (e.g., single-sentence stubs). This preprocessing pipeline is not itself a contribution of this thesis — it is inherited unchanged from the ULTRA framework, consistent with the scope defined in Section 1.8 — but is documented here for completeness, since the routing classifier's feature distributions (Chapter 5) are indirectly shaped by the characteristics of the corpus its retrieval component operates over.

[394|Normal] The resulting category distribution is visibly imbalanced, with Sports and Entertainment together accounting for over 71% of the corpus. This imbalance is a property of the underlying news source rather than a deliberate sampling choice, and it is worth flagging as a factor that could, in principle, affect retrieval quality for under-represented categories (Science & Technology, at 7.15%); this possibility is revisited as a limitation in Chapter 6.
[395|Normal] The routing classifier itself is trained on a separately curated set of 409 real Urdu and Roman Urdu queries (193 SHORT, 216 LONG). [416|Heading 2] 4.6 Confidence-Based Three-Tier Routing
[417|Caption] Table 11. Confidence-based three-tier routing strategy.
[418|Normal] HIGH confidence (≥ 85%): the query is routed directly to full semantic search against the ChromaDB index. MEDIUM confidence (≈ 60-85%): the query is routed to a hybrid search strategy, combining dense semantic retrieval with a complementary sparse lexical signal in the style of BM25 (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009), merged via a reciprocal-rank-fusion-style combination (Cormack et al., 2009) to compensate for routing uncertainty. LOW confidence (< 60%): the query triggers query expansion before retrieval, broadening the search to recover relevant results despite low routing confidence. This design ensures that the fast, direct-routing path is reserved for queries the classifier is confident about, while ambiguous queries are given additional retrieval support — retrieval effort scales inversely with routing confidence.

[452|Normal] On this development split, the dynamic classifiers achieve a 50 percentage-point improvement over the static θ = 150 baseline (100.00% vs. 50.00%). That gap is expected when the evaluation set is constructed so that true SHORT/LONG labels do not align with a 150-character cut (Figure 4.2). [453|Normal] It is worth pausing on why the static baseline lands so close to 50.00% — essentially chance-level performance for a balanced binary decision — rather than at some intermediate accuracy. Because the query dataset was deliberately curated to contain queries whose true short/long label does not correlate with the θ = 150 cut-point (Figure 4.2), the static rule's predictions are effectively uncorrelated with the ground truth on this evaluation set; 50.00% is exactly what a coin-flip decision rule would be expected to score on a class-balanced dataset. This is a stronger and more informative failure mode than a baseline that merely underperforms — it demonstrates that the static rule's decision boundary is not just miscalibrated but structurally unrelated to the actual routing requirement for this query population, which is precisely the argument made analytically in Section 4.2 with respect to Figure 4.2.
[454|Normal] The fact that both the logistic regression and SVM classifiers independently reach exactly 100.00% test accuracy is also worth interpreting statistically rather than accepting at face value. Logistic regression assumes a linear decision boundary in the (standardized) feature space, while the SVM's RBF kernel permits an arbitrarily non-linear boundary; the two models converging on identical, perfect test accuracy suggests that the eight-dimensional feature space renders the short/long distinction linearly separable — or very close to it — for this dataset, since a linear model would not match a more flexible non-linear model's accuracy otherwise. This observation is consistent with, and helps explain, the ablation results of Section 5.5, where accuracy remains unchanged after removing any single feature: a linearly (or near-linearly) separable problem in an eight-dimensional space is unlikely to depend critically on any one dimension, since redundant near-collinear features (e.g., char_length and word_count) can each individually substitute for information the others also carry.

Combined with its zero per-query operating cost, the proposed SVM classifier achieves LLM-competitive routing accuracy at local, sub-millisecond inference cost.

This variability is a further practical argument, beyond raw latency, for preferring a locally trained classifier with a known, empirically validated accuracy profile over an LLM-based router whose accuracy may vary unpredictably across providers and, plausibly, across model versions over time.

[479|Heading 2] 5.5 Ablation Study
[480|Normal] Table 5.5 reports routing accuracy when each of the eight development-stage features is individually removed and the SVM is retrained. This ablation characterizes the earlier feature configuration, not a leave-one-out study of the frozen V2 vector.
[481|Caption] Table 19. Ablation study: accuracy with each feature individually removed.
[483|Caption] Figure 10. Ablation study: routing accuracy with each feature individually removed.
[484|Normal] Every single-feature ablation on the development configuration retains 100.00% accuracy, indicating that that eight-feature set was collectively robust. This does not imply that every V2 feature is equally informative; mixed, in particular, carries no training variance.
[485|Normal] This apparent tension — a feature (has_question_words) being highly important by permutation analysis (Section 5.4) yet fully removable without any accuracy loss by ablation (this section) — is not a contradiction but a direct consequence of the two methods measuring different things. Permutation importance measures how much the model relies on a feature's specific values when all eight features are present together; ablation measures whether the model can be retrained to reach the same accuracy without that feature at all. A feature can be heavily relied upon within the full model while still being individually dispensable if other features carry enough of the same signal to substitute for it once the model is retrained. Practically, this means the eight-feature design is robust to the accidental loss or corruption of any single feature at inference time — a desirable property for a production system — without implying that all eight features are equally informative when interpreting the model's behavior.

[495|Normal] Effect size (Cohen's d). Figure 5.9 reports the standardized effect size of each feature in separating short from long queries. char_length (d=14.249) and query_length (d=11.832) show extremely large effects, alongside urdu_chars (d=1.634) and has_roman (d=0.524); the mean effect size across all measured features (d=3.581) is more than four times the conventional “large effect” threshold (d ≥ 0.8). Statistically, Cohen's d measures the standardized distance between the short-query and long-query group means for a given feature, in units of pooled standard deviation; a d of 14.249 for char_length is an extraordinarily large effect by the standards of most applied machine learning or social-science research, where d ≥ 0.8 is already considered a strong result, and indicates that the two query classes are separated by more than fourteen pooled standard deviations along this single dimension — a degree of separation that helps explain why both the linear (logistic regression) and non-linear (SVM) classifiers achieve identical, perfect accuracy (Section 5.1): the underlying classes are simply far apart in feature space, leaving little room for either model to disagree with the other or with the ground truth.

[513|Heading 2] 5.8 Error Analysis
[514|Normal] Although the development-stage routing classifier reached zero misclassifications on the primary test split (Section 5.1), the deployed V2 model is not error-free on later held-out data (98% on the 50-query diagnostic set; 86% on frozen Phase 3B). The retrieval-precision results (Section 5.2) also contain non-trivial errors, examined here rather than folded into the aggregate P@15 figures.
[515|Normal] The two lower-scoring Roman Urdu queries — “mobile phone naya model” (86.67% P@15) and “punjab police corruption” (53.33% P@15) — share a common structural pattern: each combines a term with high cross-lingual ambiguity (“mobile phone”, an English loanword commonly used as-is within Roman Urdu text; “punjab”, a proper noun that is orthographically identical in Roman transliteration and in English) with otherwise ordinary Roman Urdu vocabulary. In both cases, the transliteration dictionary (Section 4.3) is not incorrect so much as under-specified: “mobile phone” and “punjab” are valid tokens whether interpreted as English or as Roman Urdu, and the dictionary lookup step (Algorithm 4.1) has no mechanism for resolving this ambiguity contextually, since it operates at the level of individual whitespace-delimited tokens rather than the query as a whole. This is best characterized as a lexical-ambiguity error rather than a routing error: the routing classifier correctly identified both queries as Roman Urdu and routed them appropriately (Section 5.6); the precision loss originates downstream, in the transliteration step's inability to disambiguate loanwords and proper nouns from genuine Roman Urdu vocabulary.
[516|Normal] This distinction matters for interpreting the overall system's error profile: further gains in Roman Urdu P@15 are more likely to come from improving the transliteration module (e.g., a context-aware or learned transliteration model, Section 6.3) than from treating routing accuracy as a solved problem. Routing is at ceiling on some development splits, but Phase 3B shows residual SHORT/LONG errors on unseen queries (SVM: 12/16 SHORT correct, 31/34 LONG correct). Native-script Urdu queries bypass transliteration (Algorithm 4.1, Line 11) and are therefore unaffected by the loanword/proper-noun dictionary failure mode.
[517|Heading 2] 5.9 Comparison with Prior Studies
[518|Normal] Situating these results against the prior work reviewed in Chapter 2 is useful both to calibrate how large the reported improvements are in absolute terms and to identify where direct numerical comparison is, and is not, methodologically appropriate.
[519|Normal] Relative to the base ULTRA framework (Bashir et al., 2026) that this thesis directly extends, the reported native-Urdu P@15 (87.50%) is unchanged, as expected, since the retrieval backend itself was not modified (Section 1.8); the entire measured improvement (43.75% → 90.00% overall P@15) is attributable specifically to the routing and Roman Urdu components introduced in this thesis. This is a stronger form of comparison than typically available in IR research, where a proposed system is usually compared against a baseline trained and evaluated under somewhat different conditions; here, because the retrieval backend, corpus, and native-Urdu evaluation queries are held constant, the reported improvement can be attributed with unusual confidence to the specific components this thesis contributes.
[520|Normal] Relative to Arabzadeh et al.'s (2021) English sparse-versus-dense routing classifier, direct numerical comparison is not meaningful, since their evaluation uses English MS MARCO queries and a different retrieval configuration entirely; however, the qualitative finding — that a learned classifier can route queries more effectively than a fixed rule, at lower computational cost than exhaustively applying every retrieval strategy — is corroborated independently by this thesis for an unrelated language, script, and routing decision space, lending some cross-linguistic generality to the underlying premise that motivated both works.
[521|Normal] Relative to Butt et al.'s (2024) Urdu MS MARCO baseline (MRR@10 of 0.247 for their best fine-tuned configuration), no direct numerical comparison is offered in this thesis, since P@15 and MRR@10 measure different aspects of retrieval quality (precision across a result set versus rank position of the first relevant result, Section 2.4) and the two studies evaluate against different corpora and query sets. The comparison is nonetheless useful contextually: Butt et al.'s comparatively modest MRR@10, achieved with a fine-tuned multilingual transformer applying a single retrieval strategy uniformly, is consistent with this thesis's broader argument (Section 1.2) that a substantial share of Urdu IR's remaining performance gap lies not in retrieval-model quality alone but in how queries are matched to the retrieval strategy best suited to them — the specific problem this thesis's routing layer addresses.
[522|Heading 2] 5.10 Strengths and Weaknesses of the Proposed System
[523|Normal] Consolidating the results above, three strengths and three weaknesses of the proposed system are worth stating explicitly, ahead of the limitations discussion in Chapter 6.
[524|Normal] Strengths. First, routing accuracy is both very high and independently confirmed across three separate evaluation conditions (held-out test split, external validation set, and robustness checks), reducing the likelihood that the reported 100% figure is an artifact of any single evaluation procedure. Second, the system's efficiency profile — sub-millisecond inference at zero marginal cost — is not a secondary benefit but a load-bearing part of the thesis's practical argument (Section 1.3), and is demonstrated directly against real LLM-style baselines rather than assumed theoretically. Third, the Roman Urdu module measurably closes a gap (0.00% → 92.50% P@15) that the base framework did not address at all, rather than marginally improving an already-functioning capability.
[525|Normal] Weaknesses. First, as detailed in Section 5.8, the Roman Urdu transliteration module's dictionary-based design has a demonstrated failure mode on proper-noun and loanword ambiguity, placing a ceiling on Roman Urdu P@15 that routing-layer improvements alone cannot raise further. Second, even the 409-query training set and 50-query frozen primary evaluation remain modest relative to large-scale IR benchmarks; Phase 3B has low statistical power (only three discordant pairs). A larger independently annotated benchmark is needed before claiming general SVM superiority over word count. Third, the confidence-tier distribution (Section 5.6) has, to date, only been demonstrated exercising the HIGH tier; empirical validation of the MEDIUM and LOW tiers under genuinely ambiguous queries remains outstanding.
[526|Heading 2] 5.11 Practical Implications
[527|Normal] Beyond the academic contribution of demonstrating that dynamic routing outperforms static thresholding for Urdu IR, the results in this chapter carry several implications for practitioners considering deployment of a similar system. The latency and cost comparison (Section 5.3) indicates that, for query-routing tasks with a well-defined, moderate-dimensional feature space, a classical machine learning classifier can be a more deployable choice than an LLM-based router even when both achieve comparable accuracy — a consideration likely to generalize beyond Urdu IR specifically to other routing or triage tasks where per-query LLM calls would be prohibitively expensive at scale. The error analysis (Section 5.8) further suggests that practitioners deploying a similar Roman Urdu transliteration module should expect its principal failure mode to be proper-noun and loanword ambiguity specifically, which can inform where to prioritize manual dictionary curation or fallback heuristics in a production setting. Finally, the confidence-tiered architecture (Section 5.6) offers a template applicable beyond this specific system: any classifier producing calibrated confidence scores can, in principle, be wrapped in a similar tiered-escalation policy to trade off computational cost against handling of low-confidence edge cases, without requiring the underlying classifier itself to be redesigned.
[528|Heading 2] 5.12 Discussion
[529|Normal] Taken together, these results support four main conclusions. First, replacing ULTRA's static character-length threshold with a learned multi-feature SVM is technically feasible and, on development data, dramatically more accurate than θ = 150 (50.00% → 100.00%). That comparison mainly shows that θ = 150 is the wrong rule for this query population. The frozen test against a word-count baseline — the fairer simple alternative — yields only a small, non-significant difference (86.00% vs. 84.00%). Human judgments further show that word count is an imperfect proxy near five words, which motivates a learned router even when the frozen accuracy gap is small.
[530|Normal] Second, the proposed classifier matches the routing accuracy of three of four LLM-based routing baselines while operating at approximately three orders of magnitude lower latency (0.4555 ms vs. 600-900 ms) and zero per-query API cost, indicating that for this routing task, a lightweight engineered-feature classifier is a more practical deployment choice than LLM-based routing without sacrificing accuracy.
[531|Normal] Third, the Roman Urdu transliteration module extends retrieval usability to code-mixed and transliterated queries (92.50% P@15, raising overall system P@15 from 43.75% to 90.00%) that the original ULTRA framework could not process at all (0.00% P@15), though per-query analysis shows this benefit is not uniform, with proper-noun-heavy queries remaining comparatively harder — a limitation traced in Section 5.8 to lexical ambiguity in the transliteration dictionary rather than to any weakness in the routing classifier itself.
[532|Normal] Fourth, the feature importance and ablation results together reveal a nuanced picture of the classifier's internal behavior: has_question_words and lexical_diversity are the most influential features when the full model is intact, yet no single feature is individually indispensable, indicating a degree of built-in feature redundancy that contributes to the model's overall robustness rather than creating a single point of failure. Collectively, these four findings directly answer the three research questions posed in Section 1.5, and are revisited explicitly in the concluding chapter that follows.

[541|Heading 1] Chapter 6: Conclusions and Recommendations
[542|Heading 2] 6.1 Conclusions
[543|Normal] This thesis addressed a specific limitation of the ULTRA framework for Urdu information retrieval: its reliance on a single, static, character-length threshold to route queries between retrieval strategies. This static rule was replaced with a lightweight, eight-feature Support Vector Machine classifier coupled with a three-tier confidence-based escalation strategy, and paired with an expanded Roman Urdu transliteration dictionary (30 → 179 entries) to better handle code-mixed and transliterated queries. After a label audit and a 40-query gap-fill, the deployed V2 model was frozen and evaluated on an independent 60-query file.
[544|Normal] On development/cross-validation data, the proposed dynamic routing mechanism raised routing accuracy from 50.00% (static θ = 150) to 100.00% (AUC = 1.000). On the frozen 50-query primary set, accuracy is 86.00% for the V2 SVM and 84.00% for the word-count baseline (exact McNemar p = 1.0000). A diagnostic 50-query native-Urdu set yields 98.00% for V2. The classifier matched three of four LLM-style routers in a 14-query development comparison while operating at roughly three orders of magnitude lower latency (0.4555 ms vs. 600-900 ms) and no per-query API cost. The Roman Urdu module raised overall system precision (P@15) from 43.75% to 90.00% in the development retrieval experiment. Robustness checks support learnability; they do not replace the frozen test.
[545|Normal] These results answer RQ1-RQ3 with different degrees of confidence. RQ1 — whether structural and linguistic features can predict routing — is supported: the V2 feature set is internally consistent with the deployed scaler (Phase 3A) and the task is learnable on development data. RQ2 — whether dynamic routing outperforms static rules — is answered in two parts: yes, dramatically, versus ULTRA's θ = 150 on development data; only a small, non-significant descriptive edge versus a frozen word-count rule on Phase 3B. RQ3 — whether confidence-based tiered escalation improves reliability and efficiency — remains more tentative, because the demonstration batch exercised only the HIGH tier.

[556|Heading 2] 6.3 Limitations
[557|Normal] Several limitations qualify these findings and motivate the future work outlined below. These are stated candidly, in keeping with the principle that a thesis's contribution is best understood alongside an honest account of its boundaries.
[558|Normal] Dataset scale and topical scope. The 409-query training set and the 50-query frozen primary evaluation set, while diverse across topics and both scripts, are modest relative to large-scale IR benchmarks. Author-curated labels limit direct comparability with future Urdu IR work. Phase 3B's three discordant pairs imply low power to detect a small SVM-versus-word-count effect.
[559|Normal] Roman Urdu coverage. The transliteration dictionary, despite a 6x expansion, remains a finite, manually curated lookup table. Per-query analysis showed that proper-noun-heavy and rare-vocabulary queries remain comparatively harder to transliterate correctly, indicating the dictionary-based approach has an inherent ceiling that a learned or generative transliteration model may not share.
[560|Normal] Single retrieval corpus. All experiments were conducted against one news-domain corpus (111,860 Urdu news articles). The classifier's features and decision boundary were learned in this domain, and its behavior on other domains (e.g., legal, medical, or social media text) has not been evaluated.
[561|Normal] Static LLM baselines. The comparison against GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Claude, and Gemini-style routing used fixed prompting strategies rather than fine-tuned or extensively prompt-engineered variants of these models, so the reported LLM baseline numbers should be read as indicative rather than an upper bound on what LLM-based routing could achieve.
[562|Normal] Binary routing granularity. The current classifier makes a routing decision from a fixed feature set computed once per query; it does not yet incorporate user session context, query reformulation history, or multi-turn conversational signals that could further refine routing in interactive search settings.
[563|Normal] Untested confidence tiers. As noted in Section 6.1, the MEDIUM and LOW confidence tiers are implemented and formally specified (Section 3.5) but were not empirically exercised in this thesis's evaluation, since the demonstration query batch (Section 5.6) happened to fall entirely within the HIGH tier; their behavior under genuinely ambiguous or edge-case queries remains to be validated directly.
[564|Heading 2] 6.4 Recommendations for Practitioners
[565|Normal] Distinct from the research-oriented future work outlined in Section 6.5, the following recommendations are addressed specifically to practitioners considering adoption or adaptation of the methods developed in this thesis.
[566|Normal] Prefer classical ML routing over LLM-based routing for well-scoped decisions. Where a routing or triage decision can be reduced to a modest, interpretable feature set — as query routing was in this thesis — practitioners should benchmark a classical classifier (Section 2.2.2) before defaulting to an LLM-based approach, given the latency and cost advantages demonstrated in Section 5.3.
[567|Normal] Audit transliteration dictionaries for proper-noun and loanword ambiguity. Section 5.8's error analysis identified proper nouns and English loanwords as the dominant Roman Urdu error source; practitioners building similar dictionary-based transliteration modules should specifically audit and, where possible, disambiguate these categories rather than treating dictionary coverage as a uniform quantity to simply expand.
[568|Normal] Compare a learned router against the strongest simple rule, not only against a known-broken rule. ULTRA's θ = 150 character threshold is structurally misaligned with this query population; the frozen evaluation therefore uses a word-count baseline (threshold 6). Practitioners should freeze that baseline before scoring the learned model, as done in Phase 3B.
[569|Heading 2] 6.5 Recommendations for Further Research
[570|Normal] Building on the contributions and limitations above, the following directions for future research are identified.
[571|Normal] Large-scale, community benchmark construction. A natural next step is to construct and publicly release a substantially larger, independently annotated Urdu and Roman Urdu query benchmark spanning a wider range of topics, query lengths, and dialectal variation, enabling more robust cross-study comparison than is currently possible for Urdu IR.
[572|Normal] Learned or generative Roman Urdu transliteration. Rather than extending the transliteration dictionary manually, future work could train a sequence-to-sequence or transformer-based transliteration model on a larger parallel Roman Urdu-Urdu corpus, potentially closing the residual gap on proper-noun-heavy and out-of-vocabulary queries identified in Chapter 5.
[573|Normal] Cross-domain and multilingual generalization. Evaluating — and, where necessary, retraining — the dynamic routing classifier on corpora outside the news domain (e.g., government, legal, or educational text), and extending the approach to other low-resource South Asian languages that share Urdu's script and code-mixing challenges (e.g., Punjabi, Sindhi, Hindi-Urdu code-switched text), would test the generality of the proposed feature set beyond a single domain and language.
[574|Normal] Fine-tuned and prompt-optimized LLM routing. Since the current LLM comparison relies on fixed prompting strategies, a more rigorous comparison would fine-tune or systematically prompt-optimize LLM-based routers on the same training data used for the SVM classifier, providing a fairer accuracy ceiling against which the efficiency advantages of the lightweight classifier can be judged.
[575|Normal] Richer, context-aware feature sets. Incorporating session-level signals — such as prior queries in a search session, click-through feedback, or user-specific query history — into the feature set could allow the router to adapt its decisions dynamically within a single user interaction, moving from single-query classification toward session-aware retrieval routing.
[576|Normal] Dedicated stress-testing of the MEDIUM and LOW confidence tiers. As identified in Section 6.3, a targeted evaluation using deliberately ambiguous, adversarially constructed, or heavily mixed-script queries would empirically validate the hybrid-search and query-expansion tiers that this thesis's evaluation set did not happen to exercise.
[577|Normal] Deployment and real-world evaluation. Finally, integrating the proposed routing pipeline into a live Urdu search system and evaluating it against real user queries and engagement metrics (rather than offline precision measures alone) would validate whether the efficiency and accuracy gains demonstrated here translate into measurable improvements in real-world retrieval quality.
[578|Heading 2] 6.6 Real-World Applications
[579|Normal] Beyond the immediate Urdu news-retrieval setting evaluated in this thesis, the dynamic routing architecture developed here has plausible application in several adjacent, practically important settings. In consumer-facing Urdu and Roman Urdu search products — including general web search, e-commerce product search, and in-app content search for Urdu-language platforms — the routing layer could be deployed largely as-is, given its independence from the specific news-domain retrieval backend used in this thesis's evaluation (Section 1.8). In customer-support and helpdesk systems serving Urdu-speaking users, where queries are frequently typed in Roman Urdu under time pressure, the same routing-plus-transliteration architecture could reduce the proportion of queries that return no relevant results, directly improving user-facing service quality. In digital-library and government-portal search over Urdu-language documents, where query volume may be lower but computational budgets are often more constrained than in commercial search, the proposed system's zero-marginal-cost, sub-millisecond routing profile (Section 5.3) is particularly well suited relative to LLM-based alternatives that would incur recurring API costs disproportionate to typical public-sector search traffic. Realizing any of these applications would require the domain-adaptation and larger-scale validation work identified in Section 6.5, but the underlying architecture — feature-based routing plus confidence-tiered escalation — does not depend on assumptions specific to the news domain and is presented here as a general pattern applicable across these settings.
[580|Heading 2] 6.7 Closing Remarks
[581|Normal] This thesis set out to answer a narrow question — can a learned, multi-feature router outperform a static threshold for Urdu query routing — and the evidence answers it with an important qualification. Versus ULTRA's character-length rule, the learned router is clearly superior on development data. Versus a frozen word-count baseline on unseen queries, the advantage is small and not statistically significant, while human judgments still show that length is an imperfect proxy near the SHORT/LONG boundary. A second finding stands independently: a lightweight classical classifier can match LLM-style routing accuracy in a controlled development comparison at three orders of magnitude lower cost. In a research landscape oriented toward ever-larger models, that efficiency result — together with Roman Urdu coverage — is offered as a practical contribution toward more deployable Urdu information retrieval.
